Free cash flow: unlevered vs. levered, and why investors care most about it

Comes up in: Every valuation discussion, and almost every DCF question.

Cold open

"What's the difference between levered and unlevered free cash flow, and which discount rate goes with each?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

The big idea

Free cash flow is the cash a business generates after the investments it needs to keep running and growing. Unlevered free cash flow is measured before any payments to lenders, so it belongs to everyone who funds the company, debt and equity alike. Levered free cash flow is what's left for shareholders after interest and required debt repayments. Think of a rental property: the rent minus upkeep is unlevered cash flow; what's left after the mortgage payment is levered cash flow, the part that's yours.

How it works

The formulas:

  • Unlevered FCF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − capex − increase in net working capital
  • Levered FCF = net income + D&A − capex − increase in net working capital − required debt repayment

The matching rule is the part interviewers test. Unlevered free cash flow goes to all capital providers, so you discount it at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and get enterprise value. Levered free cash flow goes to shareholders, so you discount it at the cost of equity and get equity value. Bankers usually build unlevered DCFs, because the result doesn't depend on how the company happens to be financed.

In the real world

Apple is the textbook free cash flow machine. In recent fiscal years it has generated roughly $100 billion of free cash flow a year (operating cash flow above $110 billion, minus about $10 billion of capex), and it has handed most of that back through buybacks and dividends. Investors watch free cash flow because it's the cash a company can actually use: to pay down debt, return to shareholders or reinvest. For a company you follow, divide its free cash flow by its market value to get its free cash flow yield, and compare it with the 10-year Treasury yield.

The interview trap

The trap is mixing the pieces: discounting levered cash flow at WACC, or unlevered cash flow at the cost of equity.

Free cash flow is the cash a business generates after the investments it needs to sustain and grow. Unlevered free cash flow is calculated before interest: you take operating income, subtract taxes as if the company had no debt, add back depreciation and other non-cash charges, and subtract capex and the increase in working capital. Because it's available to both lenders and shareholders, you discount it at the weighted average cost of capital, which gives you enterprise value. Levered free cash flow is what's left for shareholders after interest and required debt repayments, so you discount it at the cost of equity, which gives you equity value directly. Bankers usually use unlevered free cash flow in a DCF because it doesn't depend on how the company is financed.

Your line

"Unlevered goes with WACC and enterprise value; levered goes with the cost of equity and equity value." Follow-up you'll get: "Why do bankers prefer unlevered DCFs?" The other side: For banks and other businesses where debt is part of operations, levered cash flow or dividend models often make more sense.
