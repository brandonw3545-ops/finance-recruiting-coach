## Walk me through your resume.
**Tell it as a story, not a list, in 60 to 90 seconds.** Go roughly in order: where you started, the two or three experiences that pulled you toward finance, what you did and learned in each, and why this role is the logical next step. Connect each step with *why* you moved on.

- Pick the experiences that show skills the job needs: analysis, teamwork, drive
- Have one detail ready to expand on for every line of your resume
- End on why this role, today

**Follow-up:** Which experience on your resume taught you the most, and why?

## Walk me through the three financial statements.
The **income statement** shows revenue and expenses over a period, ending in net income. The **balance sheet** is a snapshot, at one point in time, of what the company owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilities), plus shareholders' equity, and assets always equal liabilities plus equity. The **cash flow statement** starts with net income, adjusts for non-cash items like depreciation and for changes in working capital to get cash from operations, then adds cash from investing (like capital expenditures) and financing (like borrowing, repaying debt and dividends) to show the change in cash.

They link together: net income flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet and is the first line of the cash flow statement, and ending cash on the cash flow statement becomes cash on the balance sheet.

**Follow-up:** If you could use only one of the three statements to judge a company, which would you pick?

## How do the three financial statements link together?
**Net income** from the income statement is the first line of the cash flow statement, and it also flows into **retained earnings** on the balance sheet, minus any dividends. The cash flow statement then adjusts net income for **non-cash items**, like adding back depreciation, which also reduces PP&E on the balance sheet, and for **changes in working capital**, which come from balance sheet items like receivables, inventory and payables. Cash from investing ties to changes in long-term assets like PP&E, and cash from financing ties to changes in debt and equity. Finally, the **net change in cash** plus the starting cash gives the cash balance on the balance sheet, which is what makes the balance sheet balance.

**Follow-up:** Depreciation goes up by $10. Walk me through the three statements.

## Depreciation goes up by $10. Walk me through the three statements. Assume a 25% tax rate.
**Income statement:** operating income falls by $10. With a 25% tax rate, taxes fall by $2.50, so net income falls by $7.50.

**Cash flow statement:** net income is down $7.50, but depreciation is a non-cash expense, so you add back the $10. Cash from operations rises by $2.50, which is the tax saving. Cash is up $2.50.

**Balance sheet:** cash is up $2.50 and PP&E is down $10, so total assets fall by $7.50. Retained earnings fall by $7.50 with net income. Both sides are down $7.50, so it balances.

**Follow-up:** Why does cash go up when an expense went up?

## If you could use only one financial statement to judge a company, which would you pick and why?
**The cash flow statement**, because cash is the hardest number to manipulate, and it shows whether the business actually generates money. It starts with net income, so you still see profitability, then shows how much cash operations really produce after working capital, how much the company reinvests through capital expenditures, and how it's financed. That gives you free cash flow, the closest thing to a company's true earning power.

Acknowledge the trade-offs: for a bank or a company in distress, where solvency matters most, the balance sheet could be the better pick. And if you could have two, take the income statement and the balance sheet, because you can build the cash flow statement from them.

**Follow-up:** What if you could pick two?

## What's the difference between cash-basis and accrual accounting?
**Cash-basis accounting** records revenue when cash comes in and expenses when cash goes out. **Accrual accounting** records revenue when it's earned, when the product or service is delivered, and expenses when they're incurred, matched to the revenue they help produce, whenever the cash moves.

Example: you deliver $1,000 of consulting in December and get paid in February. Under accrual accounting, that's December revenue, with an account receivable until the cash arrives; under cash-basis accounting, it's February revenue. U.S. public companies must use accrual accounting under GAAP because it shows performance in each period more accurately, and that's exactly why the cash flow statement exists: to show how accrual profits turn into cash.

**Follow-up:** Give me an example of a company whose profit and cash flow look very different.

## A company buys $100 of inventory with cash. Walk me through the statements. Then it sells that inventory for $150. Walk me through again with a 25% tax rate.
**Buying the inventory:** nothing changes on the income statement. On the cash flow statement, the increase in inventory reduces cash from operations by $100. On the balance sheet, cash falls $100 and inventory rises $100, so total assets don't change.

**Selling it for $150, for cash:** on the income statement, revenue rises $150 and cost of goods sold rises $100, so pre-tax income rises $50; taxes rise $12.50 and net income rises $37.50. On the cash flow statement, net income is up $37.50 and the $100 decrease in inventory adds $100, so cash rises $137.50. On the balance sheet, cash is up $137.50 and inventory is down $100, so assets rise $37.50, and retained earnings rise $37.50. It balances.

(If the taxes haven't been paid yet, cash is up $150 and taxes payable is up $12.50. It still balances.)

**Follow-up:** What changes if the customer buys on credit instead of paying cash?

## What is working capital, and how can a growing company run out of cash even when it's profitable?
**Working capital** is current assets minus current liabilities. In operating terms, it's mostly receivables plus inventory minus payables: the cash tied up in running the business day to day.

A growing company can be profitable on paper but short of cash because it has to buy more inventory and wait to be paid by customers before it collects anything. Imagine a company growing sales from $10 million to $15 million that gives customers 60 days to pay and holds three months of inventory, but pays its suppliers in 30 days. Every extra dollar of sales ties up more cash in receivables and inventory. The profit shows up on the income statement, but the cash is stuck on the balance sheet. If the company can't borrow or raise equity to cover the gap, it can run out of cash while reporting record profits. That's why lenders watch the cash conversion cycle, and why fast growth can be dangerous.

**Follow-up:** How could the company fix that cash crunch without slowing its growth?

## What's the difference between net income and free cash flow?
**Net income** is accounting profit: revenue minus all expenses, including non-cash ones like depreciation, under accrual rules. **Free cash flow** is the cash the business actually generates after investing to maintain and grow itself: cash from operations minus capital expenditures.

To get from net income to free cash flow, add back non-cash expenses like depreciation and stock-based compensation, subtract increases in working capital, and subtract capital expenditures. The two can be far apart: a fast-growing company investing heavily can have positive net income but negative free cash flow, while a mature company with little to reinvest can generate free cash flow well above its net income. Investors often trust free cash flow more because it's harder to manipulate.

**Follow-up:** Which matters more when you value a company, and why?

## How does stock-based compensation show up on the three statements?
**Income statement:** it's an operating expense, spread across lines like R&D and sales and marketing, so it lowers operating income and net income. It's generally tax-deductible, though the timing can differ.

**Cash flow statement:** it's a non-cash expense, so it's added back in cash from operations, like depreciation.

**Balance sheet:** with $10 of stock-based compensation and a 25% tax rate, net income falls $7.50 and the $10 add-back means cash rises $2.50. On the other side, retained earnings fall $7.50 while paid-in capital rises $10, so equity rises $2.50. It balances.

The debate: adding it back makes cash flow look stronger, but it's a real cost to shareholders, paid through dilution. Many analysts subtract it when they calculate free cash flow, or at least use the fully diluted share count.

**Follow-up:** Should you add back stock-based compensation when calculating EBITDA or free cash flow?

## What is goodwill, and when is it created?
**Goodwill** is created when one company buys another for more than the fair value of its identifiable net assets. It represents things that aren't recorded separately, like the assembled workforce, reputation and expected synergies.

Example: a buyer pays $1 billion for a company whose identifiable assets are worth $700 million at fair value, with $100 million of liabilities. Net identifiable assets are $600 million, so goodwill is $400 million. In practice, part of the excess first goes to identifiable intangible assets like customer relationships or patents, which are amortized.

Under U.S. GAAP, public companies don't amortize goodwill. They test it for impairment at least once a year, and if the acquired business is worth less than its value on the books, they record a write-down: a non-cash expense that reduces net income.

**Follow-up:** What happens on the three statements when goodwill is impaired?

## A company raises $100 of debt and uses it to buy PP&E. Walk me through the statements at the moment of purchase.
**Income statement:** nothing changes yet. There's no interest or depreciation at the moment of purchase.

**Cash flow statement:** cash from financing is +$100 from the debt, and cash from investing is −$100 for the capital expenditure, so the net change in cash is zero.

**Balance sheet:** PP&E rises $100 on the assets side and debt rises $100 on the liabilities side; cash doesn't change. Both sides rise $100, so it balances.

Over the following year, interest expense and depreciation would reduce net income, depreciation would be added back on the cash flow statement, and any debt repayment would show up in financing.

## Why is EBITDA so widely used, and what are its flaws?
**Why it's used:** EBITDA, earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, is a rough proxy for operating cash flow that isn't affected by how a company is financed, its tax situation or its depreciation policies, so it makes companies easier to compare. It pairs with enterprise value in EV/EBITDA, and lenders use it in leverage ratios like debt to EBITDA.

**Its flaws:**
- It ignores capital expenditures, which can be huge for capital-intensive businesses. Warren Buffett once asked whether management thinks "the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures."
- It ignores changes in working capital.
- It ignores interest and taxes, which are real cash costs.
- "Adjusted EBITDA" often strips out recurring costs like stock-based compensation or "one-time" restructuring charges, which can flatter results.
- It isn't defined under GAAP, so companies calculate it differently.

**Follow-up:** For what kind of company would EBITDA be most misleading?

## A company writes down $50 of inventory. Walk me through the statements with a 25% tax rate.
**Income statement:** the write-down is an expense, often recorded in cost of goods sold, so pre-tax income falls $50. Taxes fall $12.50, so net income falls $37.50.

**Cash flow statement:** net income is down $37.50, but the write-down is non-cash, so you add back $50. Cash from operations rises $12.50, from the tax saving.

**Balance sheet:** cash rises $12.50 and inventory falls $50, so assets fall $37.50. Retained earnings fall $37.50 with net income, so it balances.

It follows the same pattern as an increase in depreciation: a non-cash expense that saves taxes.

**Follow-up:** Why would a company write down inventory, and what might it signal to investors?

## What is deferred revenue, and why might a fast-growing subscription company have a lot of it?
**Deferred revenue** is cash a company has collected for products or services it hasn't delivered yet. It's a liability, because the company still owes the customer the service, and it turns into revenue as the service is delivered.

Example: a software company bills a customer $1,200 upfront for a one-year subscription. It gets the cash on day one, records $1,200 of deferred revenue, and recognizes $100 of revenue each month.

A fast-growing subscription company builds up a lot of it because it keeps signing new annual contracts that are paid upfront, so cash arrives ahead of revenue. That's a strong position: customers are effectively financing the business, so cash flow can run well ahead of reported profit. A growing deferred revenue balance is also a sign of revenue already locked in for the future.

**Follow-up:** How does an increase in deferred revenue show up on the cash flow statement?

## Walk me through a DCF.
A DCF values a company based on the present value of its future cash flows.

1. **Project unlevered free cash flow** for five to ten years: revenue growth and margins to get operating income, then subtract taxes, add back depreciation, and subtract capital expenditures and the investment in working capital.
2. **Calculate a terminal value** for the years after that, using the perpetuity growth method or an exit multiple of EBITDA.
3. **Discount** the cash flows and the terminal value back to today at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), with the cost of equity from CAPM and the after-tax cost of debt.
4. **Add them up** to get enterprise value.
5. **Bridge to equity value** by subtracting net debt and other claims, then divide by diluted shares for an implied share price.
6. **Run sensitivity tables** on the discount rate and the terminal value assumptions.

Aim to say it in under 60 seconds.

**Follow-up:** What happens to the valuation if interest rates rise?

## What's the difference between equity value and enterprise value?
**Equity value** is the value of a company's shares: the share price times diluted shares outstanding, the part that belongs to shareholders. **Enterprise value** is the value of the company's core operations to all of its capital providers: equity value plus debt, preferred stock and noncontrolling interests, minus cash.

Think of a house worth $500,000 with a $400,000 mortgage: the owner's equity is $100,000, but the house itself is worth $500,000. We use enterprise value to compare companies with different amounts of debt, and we pair it with metrics that belong to all investors, like revenue or EBITDA. Equity value pairs with metrics that belong only to shareholders, like net income.

**Follow-up:** Can enterprise value be lower than equity value?

## Why do you add debt and subtract cash to get from equity value to enterprise value?
Enterprise value is the value of the core business to everyone with a claim on it. If you bought the whole company, you'd pay the shareholders the equity value, but you'd also have to take on or repay its debt, so you add debt, along with other investor claims like preferred stock and noncontrolling interests.

You subtract cash because it isn't part of the operating business, and you'd get it along with the company: you could use it to pay down the debt, so it reduces your real cost. That's also why enterprise value doesn't change when a company borrows money and holds it as cash: debt and cash rise by the same amount.

**Follow-up:** A company borrows $100 and holds it as cash. What happens to enterprise value?

## What are the main ways to value a company, and which usually gives the highest value?
There are three main methods, plus a couple used in specific situations:

- **Comparable companies (comps):** apply the multiples that similar public companies trade at.
- **Precedent transactions:** apply the multiples paid in past acquisitions of similar companies.
- **Discounted cash flow (DCF):** the present value of projected free cash flows plus a terminal value.
- **LBO analysis:** what a private equity firm could pay and still earn its target return; it often sets a floor.
- **Sum of the parts:** valuing each division separately, for companies with distinct businesses.

**Precedent transactions usually give the highest value**, because buyers pay a control premium, often 20% to 40% over the share price, for control and synergies. A DCF can come out higher or lower depending on its assumptions, and it usually has the widest range. Comps reflect today's market prices, with no premium. Bankers show the methods side by side in a "football field" chart.

**Follow-up:** Why might a DCF give a very different value from comps?

## How do you calculate WACC, and why is the cost of equity usually higher than the cost of debt?
WACC is the weighted average of what each type of investor requires: the cost of equity times equity's share of total capital, plus the after-tax cost of debt times debt's share, using market values for the weights.

The cost of equity usually comes from CAPM: the risk-free rate, typically the 10-year Treasury yield, plus beta times the equity risk premium. The cost of debt is what the company pays to borrow, multiplied by one minus the tax rate, because interest is tax-deductible. Example: a 10% cost of equity, a 4.5% after-tax cost of debt and a 70/30 mix give a WACC of 8.35%.

Equity costs more than debt because shareholders are paid last and have no guaranteed return, so they demand more, and the tax deduction makes debt cheaper still. That's also why adding debt lowers WACC only up to a point: past that, lenders and shareholders both see more risk and raise their required returns.

**Follow-up:** What happens to WACC as a company takes on more debt?

## What's the difference between levered and unlevered free cash flow, and which discount rate goes with each?
**Unlevered free cash flow** is the cash the business generates before any payments to lenders: EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + depreciation and amortization − capital expenditures − the increase in working capital. It belongs to all capital providers, so you discount it at **WACC**, and the result is **enterprise value**.

**Levered free cash flow** is what's left for shareholders after interest and required debt repayments: start from net income, which is already after interest, add back depreciation, subtract capital expenditures and the working capital investment, and subtract mandatory debt repayments. It belongs to shareholders, so you discount it at the **cost of equity**, and the result is **equity value**.

The rule: match the cash flow to the investors it belongs to, and the discount rate to what those investors require. Unlevered DCFs are more common because they don't depend on how the company happens to be financed.

**Follow-up:** Why do bankers usually use unlevered free cash flow?

## How would you value a company that has no earnings yet?
Use measures that exist before profits do, and anchor on where the business is heading:

- **Revenue multiples:** EV/Revenue or EV/gross profit against comparable companies, adjusted for growth; for software, recurring revenue.
- **Operating metrics:** value per user, subscriber or customer, if peers trade on those.
- **A DCF with a longer projection period:** project until margins mature, often ten years, then add a terminal value, with heavy sensitivity analysis, since most of the value is far in the future.
- **Precedent transactions and recent funding rounds** in the same space.
- **The venture capital method:** estimate what the company could sell for in five to seven years, then discount that back at a high target return, like 30% to 50%.

Also look at unit economics, like what it costs to win a customer compared with what that customer is worth over time, and at cash runway, since a company with no earnings has to fund itself until it gets there.

**Follow-up:** Which of those methods would you trust most for an early-stage software company, and why?

## What is terminal value, and what are the two ways to calculate it?
Terminal value captures the value of all the cash flows after the projection period, since a company doesn't stop operating after five or ten years.

- **Perpetuity growth method:** take the final year's free cash flow, grow it one more year at a long-term growth rate, and divide by the discount rate minus that growth rate. The growth rate should be modest, usually 2% to 3%, below long-run economic growth.
- **Exit multiple method:** apply a multiple, like EV/EBITDA from comparable companies, to the final year's EBITDA.

Either way, you then discount the terminal value back to today. It often makes up well over half of a DCF's value, so analysts cross-check the two methods (what multiple does the perpetuity value imply?) and run sensitivity tables on the growth rate, the multiple and the discount rate.

## Walk me through a basic LBO.
In a leveraged buyout, a private equity firm buys a company using a large amount of borrowed money, often half or more of the price, plus its own equity, then aims to sell it in three to seven years.

1. **Purchase:** set the price, usually as a multiple of EBITDA, and the sources and uses of funds: debt, the sponsor's equity and fees.
2. **Projections:** project revenue, EBITDA and free cash flow over the holding period.
3. **Debt paydown:** use the free cash flow to pay interest and repay debt each year.
4. **Exit:** assume a sale, typically at an EBITDA multiple, to get the enterprise value at exit, then subtract the remaining net debt to get the equity value.
5. **Returns:** compare the exit equity with the equity invested to get the multiple of money (MOIC) and the IRR. Firms often target IRRs of 20% or more, or roughly 2.5 times their money over five years.

Returns come from three places: EBITDA growth, debt paydown and a higher exit multiple. The debt magnifies the return on the equity, in both directions.

**Follow-up:** Why does using more debt increase the returns, and what's the risk?

## What makes a company a good LBO candidate?
A good LBO candidate can carry a lot of debt safely and grow its equity value:

- **Stable, predictable cash flows** to pay interest and repay debt
- **Low capital expenditure needs** and modest working capital requirements, so cash is free for debt payments
- **A strong market position** with steady demand, often in a mature industry
- **Little existing debt**, and ideally assets that can serve as collateral
- **Room to improve:** cutting costs, raising margins or buying smaller competitors
- **A strong management team**
- **A sensible purchase price** and a realistic exit: a sale to a company, another firm or an IPO

A fast-growing startup burning cash is usually a poor candidate; a steady business services company with recurring revenue is a classic one.

## A company with a P/E of 20 buys a company with a P/E of 10 using all stock. Is the deal accretive or dilutive, and why?
**Accretive**, ignoring any premium, fees and synergies. In an all-stock deal, the buyer pays with its own shares, and the "cost" of those shares is its earnings yield: 1 ÷ 20 = 5%. It's buying earnings at an earnings yield of 1 ÷ 10 = 10%. Since it's buying earnings more cheaply than the earnings each new share gives up, earnings per share rise.

Quick proof: the buyer has 100 shares at $20 and $100 of earnings (EPS of $1, P/E of 20). The target has $50 of earnings and is worth $500 (P/E of 10). The buyer issues 25 shares ($500 ÷ $20). New EPS = $150 ÷ 125 shares = $1.20, up 20%.

Rule of thumb: in an all-stock deal, if the buyer's P/E is higher than the P/E it pays for the target, the deal is accretive. In practice, the premium, fees and synergies all change the answer.

**Follow-up:** What if the buyer paid a 50% premium?

## Why do acquirers usually pay a premium over the current share price?
Because they're buying **control**, and shareholders won't sell for less than they could get in the market.

- **Control:** owning 100% lets the buyer change the strategy, management, capital structure and how cash is used, which is worth more than a minority stake.
- **Synergies:** cost savings or extra revenue from combining the businesses, part of which the buyer shares with the seller to win the deal.
- **Persuasion:** the price has to beat what shareholders and the board think the stock is worth on its own, and fend off rival bidders.

Premiums of roughly 20% to 40% over the undisturbed share price are common, which is why precedent transactions usually imply higher values than trading comps. The risk is overpaying: if the synergies don't show up, the premium destroys value for the buyer's shareholders.

**Follow-up:** How would you decide how much of the expected synergies to pay away in the premium?

## What are the limits of the P/E ratio?
- **Useless when earnings are negative or tiny,** which rules out many young companies and cyclical ones at the bottom of their cycle.
- **Distorted by capital structure:** net income is after interest, so two identical businesses with different amounts of debt have different P/Es. EV/EBITDA avoids that.
- **Affected by accounting choices and one-time items,** like write-downs, gains on asset sales, tax quirks and depreciation policies.
- **Says nothing about growth or risk on its own:** a high P/E can be cheap if growth is fast. The PEG ratio, P/E divided by growth, tries to adjust for that.
- **Misleading for cyclical companies:** they often look cheapest on P/E at the peak of the cycle, just before earnings fall.
- **Ignores cash flow:** earnings aren't cash, especially with heavy capital spending or working capital needs.

That's why analysts use it alongside EV/EBITDA, free cash flow yield and a DCF.

**Follow-up:** When would P/E be the best multiple to use?

## Company A trades at 10x EBITDA and Company B at 15x. What could explain the gap?
A higher multiple means investors expect more or worry less. Company B might have:

- **Faster expected growth** in revenue and earnings
- **Higher or more stable margins,** or more recurring revenue
- **Better returns on capital and lower capital spending needs,** so more of its EBITDA becomes free cash flow
- **Lower risk:** less cyclical demand, less debt, a stronger competitive position or management
- **A different business mix,** with part of it in a more highly valued segment

Or the gap could be temporary: A's EBITDA might be inflated by a one-time boost, or B's temporarily depressed, or B might be the subject of takeover talk or simply in fashion.

To check, compare growth forecasts, margins, capital spending relative to EBITDA, debt and free cash flow conversion. If the gap isn't justified, that's a potential investment idea.

**Follow-up:** How would you decide whether A is a bargain or deserves the lower multiple?

## If interest rates rise, what happens to a DCF valuation, and why?
The valuation usually **falls**. Higher rates raise the risk-free rate, which raises the cost of equity through CAPM, and they raise the cost of debt, so WACC rises. Future cash flows are discounted more heavily, and the terminal value falls the most, because it's furthest away and very sensitive to the gap between WACC and the growth rate.

Companies whose value lies far in the future, like high-growth technology companies, are hit hardest, which is a big part of why growth stocks fell so sharply in 2022. A complete answer adds the second-order effects: higher rates can slow the economy and cut the cash flow projections themselves, raise interest costs and lower exit multiples. The partial offset: if rates rise because growth and inflation are strong, nominal cash flows may grow faster too.

**Follow-up:** Which companies would be hurt most, and which might benefit?

## Paper LBO: you buy a company with $100M of EBITDA at 10x using 60% debt. Five years later EBITDA is $150M, you exit at 10x, and $200M of debt has been paid down. What are the MOIC and the rough IRR?
**Entry:** 10 × $100M = a $1,000M purchase price, funded with $600M of debt and $400M of equity.

**Exit:** 10 × $150M = $1,500M of enterprise value. Debt is down to $600M − $200M = $400M, so the equity is worth $1,500M − $400M = $1,100M.

**MOIC:** $1,100M ÷ $400M = **2.75x**.

**IRR:** about **22%**. Shortcut: over five years, 2x is roughly 15%, 2.5x roughly 20% and 3x roughly 25%, so 2.75x lands around 22% to 23%. (Exactly, 2.75 to the power of 1/5, minus 1, is about 22.4%.)

Where the return came from: EBITDA growth added $500M of value and debt paydown $200M, with no help from the multiple. This ignores fees and any cash paid out along the way.

## How do interest rates affect stock prices?
Higher rates hurt stock prices through three channels.

1. **Valuation:** interest rates are the base of the discount rate, so when they rise, future profits are worth less today. Companies whose profits are far in the future, like high-growth tech, get hit hardest.
2. **Competition:** when safe Treasuries pay more, investors demand a higher expected return from stocks, and prices fall until they offer it.
3. **The economy:** higher borrowing costs slow consumer spending and business investment and raise companies' interest expense, which cuts into earnings.

But it depends on why rates are moving and what was expected. If rates rise because growth is strong, stocks can still do fine, and markets react to the expected path of rates more than to any single decision. Banks can even benefit, while utilities and real estate, which behave like bonds, usually suffer.

**Follow-up:** Why did growth stocks fall so much more than value stocks in 2022?

## What's the relationship between bond prices and yields? Explain it with an example.
They move in opposite directions, because a bond's payments are fixed. If I own a bond with a 4% coupon, paying $40 a year on $1,000, and market rates rise to 5%, new buyers can get 5% on newly issued bonds. They'll only buy mine at a discount, low enough that the fixed $40 a year plus the gain back to $1,000 adds up to a 5% return. For a 10-year bond, that's a price of about $923. If rates fall to 3%, the reverse happens, and the same bond is worth about $1,085.

The effect is bigger for longer bonds, because more of their cash flows are far in the future. That's what duration measures: a bond with a duration of about 8 loses roughly 8% of its value for a one-point rise in rates.

**Follow-up:** If you expect rates to fall, would you rather own a 2-year or a 30-year bond?

## What does an inverted yield curve signal, and how reliable has that signal been?
The yield curve plots Treasury yields across maturities. Normally it slopes upward, because investors want extra yield for lending for longer. It inverts when short-term yields rise above long-term ones, which usually happens when the Fed has raised short-term rates to fight inflation while investors expect growth to slow and rates to come down later.

**What it signals:** markets expect the Fed to cut, usually because of a slowdown, and it squeezes banks, which borrow short and lend long, so credit can tighten.

**How reliable:** before 2022, an inversion had come before every U.S. recession since the 1950s, with one false alarm in the mid-1960s, usually six months to two years ahead. But it's a signal, not a cause or a timer. The 10-year minus 2-year spread inverted in July 2022 and stayed inverted for about two years, yet the economy kept growing through 2023 and 2024. Some blame pandemic-era distortions and the Fed's bond buying; others note that recessions often begin after the curve turns positive again.

**Follow-up:** Why might the signal have failed, or been delayed, this time?

## What does the Fed do, and how does a rate cut ripple through markets?
The Federal Reserve is the U.S. central bank, with a dual mandate from Congress: maximum employment and stable prices, which it defines as 2% inflation. Its main tool is the federal funds rate, which the FOMC sets as a target range at eight meetings a year. It can also buy or shrink its holdings of bonds and use forward guidance.

**When it cuts:**
- Short-term rates fall right away: Treasury bills, savings accounts, credit cards and floating-rate loans.
- Longer-term rates often fall too, if markets expect more cuts, lowering mortgage and corporate borrowing costs.
- Stocks often rise, because future cash flows are worth more and borrowing is cheaper, especially growth stocks and rate-sensitive sectors like real estate.
- The dollar tends to weaken.
- Over time, cheaper credit supports spending, hiring and investment.

The caveat: markets move on expectations. A cut that's already priced in may do little, and a cut paired with a signal of fewer cuts ahead can send stocks down, as on December 18, 2024.

**Follow-up:** Why might the 10-year Treasury yield rise even after the Fed cuts?

## If inflation comes in hotter than expected, what happens to stocks, bonds and the dollar?
The typical first reaction:

- **Bonds fall and yields rise,** especially short-term yields, because markets expect the Fed to keep rates higher for longer, or even raise them.
- **Stocks fall,** because higher expected rates raise discount rates and threaten growth. Growth and tech stocks and rate-sensitive sectors usually fall most, while energy and commodity producers may hold up better.
- **The dollar rises,** because higher expected U.S. interest rates attract money from abroad.

The key word is *expected*: markets react to the surprise compared with forecasts, not the level itself. The details matter too. A hot number driven by volatile items may be shrugged off, while hot core or services inflation worries the Fed more. In 2022, repeated inflation surprises drove stocks and bonds down together.

## Why does the 10-year Treasury yield matter so much?
It's the benchmark long-term interest rate for the whole economy.

- **Borrowing costs:** 30-year mortgage rates track it closely, and corporate bonds are priced as a spread over Treasuries.
- **Valuation:** it's the usual risk-free rate in the cost of equity and WACC, so it sets the starting point for discount rates. When it rises, the present value of future cash flows falls.
- **Signals:** it reflects markets' expectations for growth, inflation and Fed policy, and its gap with the 2-year yield is the slope of the yield curve.
- **Global money flows:** it's the reference rate for the world's largest bond market, so it moves the dollar and markets abroad.

Unlike the fed funds rate, the Fed doesn't set it; markets do. Know its level before any interview.

## How does a strong dollar affect a U.S. company that sells mostly overseas?
It usually **hurts reported results.** The company earns revenue in euros, yen or other currencies, and when the dollar strengthens, each unit of foreign currency converts into fewer dollars. Reported revenue and earnings fall even if sales haven't changed in local currency. It can also hurt competitiveness: if the company makes products in the U.S. and prices them in dollars, they get more expensive for foreign customers.

Example: a company sells €100 million of products in Europe. At $1.10 per euro, that's $110 million; if the dollar strengthens to $1.00 per euro, it's $100 million, a 9% drop in reported revenue with no change in the business.

The offsets: if many of its costs are also in foreign currencies, margins are partly protected, and many companies hedge their currency exposure. That's why companies report growth in "constant currency," and analysts watch it to see the underlying trend.

## Pitch me a stock.
**A pitch is an argument, not a company profile.** In about two minutes:

## What's the difference between a call and a put?
Both are contracts that give the buyer a right, not an obligation. A **call** gives the right to buy a stock at a set strike price before expiration, so it gains value when the stock rises. A **put** gives the right to sell at the strike, so it gains value when the stock falls. The buyer pays a premium, which is the most they can lose, while the seller collects the premium and takes on the obligation if the option is exercised.

For example, a call with a $105 strike bought for $3 makes money only if the stock finishes above $108. Investors use calls to bet on a rise with limited downside, and puts to protect a position they own, much like buying insurance.

**Follow-up:** What happens to an option's value when volatility rises?

## What happens to the price of an option when volatility rises? Why?
**Both calls and puts become more valuable.** An option's payoff is lopsided: the buyer's loss is capped at the premium, but the gain grows with the size of the move. Higher volatility means bigger possible moves in both directions. The bigger moves in the option's favor help the buyer a lot, while the bigger moves against it can't cost more than the premium. So more volatility raises the option's expected payoff, and its price.

The sensitivity of an option's price to volatility is called **vega**, and it's largest for at-the-money options with a long time left until expiration. Traders often quote options in terms of implied volatility, the volatility that makes the market price fit a pricing model like Black–Scholes; buying options is effectively buying volatility.

**Follow-up:** What's implied volatility, and how is it different from historical volatility?

## What is delta, in plain English?
**Delta is how much an option's price changes when the underlying stock moves by $1.** A call with a delta of 0.5 gains about 50 cents if the stock rises $1. Calls have deltas between 0 and 1, and puts between −1 and 0.

- Deep in-the-money options have deltas near 1 (or −1 for puts): they move almost one-for-one with the stock.
- Far out-of-the-money options have deltas near 0.
- At-the-money options have deltas around 0.5 (or −0.5).

Delta is also a rough guide to the chance the option finishes in the money, and it tells you the hedge: a trader who sold 100 call contracts, each on 100 shares, with a delta of 0.5, would buy about 5,000 shares to be "delta neutral." Delta changes as the stock moves, and the rate of that change is called **gamma**.

## Why would a company use an interest rate swap?
**To change its interest rate exposure without refinancing its debt.** In a plain vanilla swap, two parties exchange interest payments on a notional amount: one pays a fixed rate, and the other pays a floating rate, like SOFR.

Example: a company has a $100 million floating-rate loan at SOFR plus 2% and worries that rates will rise. It enters a swap in which it pays a fixed 4% and receives SOFR. The SOFR it receives offsets the SOFR it pays on the loan, so its all-in cost is locked at about 6%: 4% plus 2%.

Reasons to do it: to make interest costs predictable, to match assets and liabilities (as banks and insurers do), to borrow where it's cheapest and swap into the exposure it wants, or to express a view on rates. The risks: if rates fall, the company misses out on the savings, and the other side of the swap could fail to pay.

**Follow-up:** If rates fall after the company enters the swap, who wins and who loses?

**Sample:** "If I thought inflation would cool faster than the market expects, I'd buy 2-year Treasuries, because the 2-year yield is the most sensitive to Fed expectations. The catalyst is the next CPI report. I'd get out if the 2-year yield rose another quarter of a point."

Show that you know current levels and think in terms of risk and reward, not just direction.

**Follow-up:** How would you size that trade, and where's your stop?

## You're making a market in a stock trading around $50. How do you set your bid and ask, and what moves them?
**Quote a two-sided price around your estimate of fair value, wide enough to earn a profit and cover your risk:** for example, a bid of $49.98 and an offer of $50.02, in a size you're comfortable with.

**What widens the spread:** higher volatility, lower trading volume, a bigger order, upcoming news like earnings, and the risk that the person trading with you knows something you don't.

**What moves your quotes:** new information, like news or moves in related stocks and index futures; the orders you see; and your own inventory. If you've bought a lot, you're long and want to cut your risk, so you lower both prices, say to $49.96 and $50.00, to attract buyers and discourage more sellers. If you're short, you raise them.

A market maker earns the spread across many trades and tries not to bet on direction, managing its inventory by adjusting quotes or hedging.

**Follow-up:** Someone asks you to buy a very large block. How does that change your price?

## Would you rather have $1 million for sure or a 50% chance at $2.5 million? Walk me through how you think about it.
**Start with expected value, then explain why it isn't the whole answer.** The gamble's expected value is 0.5 × $2.5 million = $1.25 million, more than the sure $1 million, so someone who only cared about expected value would take the gamble.

But most people should take **the $1 million**, because each extra dollar matters less the more you have: going from nothing to $1 million changes your life far more than going from $1 million to $2.5 million, so a 50% chance of ending up with nothing isn't worth an extra $250,000 of expected value. The answer can change with circumstances: someone already wealthy might take the gamble, and if you could play many times, the gamble wins. You could even sell the gamble to someone with more money for more than $1 million.

The interviewer wants to see you calculate the expected value, think about risk and make a clear decision.

**Follow-up:** At what guaranteed amount would you be indifferent?

## How would you explain diversification to a client with no finance background?
**Use a simple analogy and no jargon, then connect it to their goals.**

**Sample:** "Think of a shop that sells both umbrellas and sunscreen. On rainy days the umbrellas sell, and on sunny days the sunscreen does, so the shop has steady income whatever the weather. Your portfolio works the same way: we hold different kinds of investments that tend to do well at different times, so when one has a bad year, the others steady the whole. It doesn't mean you'll never lose money, but it means one bad company or one bad market won't wreck your plans. It's also why we don't put everything into whatever did best last year."

Then check that they understood, and tie it back to their own portfolio and goals.

**Follow-up:** The client asks, "Then why don't I just buy the best-performing fund?" What do you say?

## A 65-year-old client wants to move all their savings into tech stocks. What do you say?
**Don't just say no. Understand why, explain the risk in terms of their goals and offer a better path.**

1. **Ask why:** are they worried about falling behind, excited by recent returns, or following a friend?
2. **Connect it to their situation:** at 65, they'll probably be drawing on their savings soon. Tech stocks can fall hard: the Nasdaq fell about 33% in 2022 and about 78% from 2000 to 2002, and they may not have time to wait for a recovery. Selling in a downturn to pay living expenses would lock in the losses.
3. **Show the numbers:** what a 40% drop would mean for their income and retirement plans.
4. **Offer a middle ground:** a modest, deliberate slice of tech within a diversified portfolio, with several years of spending covered by safer assets.
5. **Document the conversation,** and if they insist, follow the firm's procedures for trades the client directs.

The interviewer is testing empathy, judgment and your duty to act in the client's interest.

**Follow-up:** The client insists. What do you do?

## What's the difference between active and passive management, and why do most active managers trail their benchmark?
**Passive** management tracks an index, like the S&P 500, at very low cost, accepting the market's return. **Active** management picks securities to try to beat a benchmark, and charges higher fees for the research and trading.

Most active managers trail their benchmark because of simple arithmetic, laid out by William Sharpe: before costs, active investors as a group hold the market, so on average they earn the market's return. After fees and trading costs, the average active dollar must fall short. Markets are also competitive, so mispricings get spotted quickly, and managers who do beat the index rarely keep it up. S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA scorecard found that about 90% of U.S. large-cap active funds trailed the S&P 500 over the 15 years through 2024.

Active management has its best case in less efficient markets like small companies, emerging markets and credit, for tax management or particular client needs, and markets need active investors to set prices.

**Follow-up:** Where do you think active management has the best chance of adding value?

## What's the difference between a fiduciary and a broker?
A **fiduciary**, such as a registered investment adviser, has a legal duty to put the client's interests first at all times: advice must be in the client's best interest, conflicts must be avoided or fully disclosed, and the duty continues for as long as the relationship lasts. Fiduciaries are usually paid a fee, often a percentage of assets, rather than commissions.

A **broker**, a registered representative of a broker-dealer, was historically held to a "suitability" standard: recommendations had to be suitable for the client, not necessarily the best option. Since 2020, the SEC's **Regulation Best Interest** has required brokers to act in a retail customer's best interest when making a recommendation and to disclose conflicts, but the duty applies at the moment of the recommendation rather than on an ongoing basis, and brokers are often paid by commission.

Many advisors are registered as both, so a client should ask which role the advisor is playing for each piece of advice.

**Follow-up:** Why might a client prefer one over the other?

## What is the Sharpe ratio, and what does it miss?
The **Sharpe ratio** measures return per unit of risk: (portfolio return − risk-free rate) ÷ the portfolio's volatility, measured as the standard deviation of its returns. A fund that returns 10% when Treasury bills pay 4%, with 15% volatility, has a Sharpe ratio of (10% − 4%) ÷ 15% = 0.4. Higher is better, and it lets you compare portfolios that take different amounts of risk.

**What it misses:**
- It treats upside and downside swings the same; the Sortino ratio penalizes only the downside.
- It assumes returns are roughly normally distributed, so it misses tail risk: a strategy that earns small, steady gains and then crashes can look great until it doesn't.
- It can be flattered by assets that are priced infrequently, whose returns look smoother than they really are.
- It depends heavily on the time period measured.
- It ignores how the investment fits with the rest of a portfolio.

**Follow-up:** Fund A has a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 and Fund B has 0.5. Is A always the better choice?

## How would you build a portfolio for a 25-year-old saving for retirement?
**Start with their goals and situation, then build something simple, diversified and cheap.**

1. **Foundation first:** an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses in cash, and pay off any high-interest debt.
2. **Use tax-advantaged accounts:** contribute enough to the 401(k) to get the full employer match, then a Roth IRA, since their tax rate is probably low now, then more 401(k).
3. **Allocation:** mostly stocks, perhaps 80% to 90%, in U.S. and international index funds, because they have about 40 years to ride out downturns and need growth; 10% to 20% in bonds if it helps them stay calm in a crash. A target-date fund does this automatically.
4. **Keep costs low:** index funds and ETFs with low expense ratios.
5. **Automate and rebalance:** contribute every paycheck, rebalance once a year, and shift gradually toward bonds as retirement gets closer.

The biggest risk for a young investor isn't volatility; it's panic-selling or not saving enough.

**Follow-up:** How would the portfolio change if they told you they'd panic-sell in a 30% drop?

## What's the difference between a Roth IRA and a traditional IRA, and who should pick which?
**Traditional IRA:** contributions may be tax-deductible now, depending on income and whether you have a workplace plan; the money grows tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Required minimum distributions start in your 70s.

**Roth IRA:** contributions go in after tax, with no deduction now; the money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. The original owner never has to take required minimum distributions, and contributions (but not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time. There are income limits on contributing directly.

**Who should pick which:** it mostly comes down to whether your tax rate is higher now or in retirement. If you expect a higher rate later, as most students and early-career workers should, a **Roth** usually wins. If you're in a high bracket now and expect a lower one in retirement, the **traditional** IRA's deduction is worth more. Many people use both to spread out their future tax exposure.

**Follow-up:** Can a high earner still get money into a Roth? How?

## How do wealth managers get paid, and what conflicts can that create?
**How they're paid, and the conflict each can create:**
- **A fee as a percentage of assets,** often around 1% a year: it ties pay to growing the account, but it can discourage advice that shrinks the account, like paying off a mortgage or buying an annuity elsewhere.
- **Commissions** on trades or products like insurance and annuities: an incentive to recommend high-commission products or to trade more than necessary.
- **Flat or hourly planning fees:** fewer conflicts, but often less ongoing involvement.
- **Proprietary products and revenue sharing:** the firm may earn more when clients use its own funds.
- **Lending and banking:** interest on mortgages or loans against a portfolio.

**How conflicts are managed:** disclosure (in documents like Form CRS and Form ADV), fiduciary and best-interest standards, fee-only models and firm compliance. A strong answer admits that every model has some conflict and explains how a good advisor puts the client first anyway.

**Follow-up:** Which pay model do you think best aligns the advisor with the client, and why?

## A client panics after the market drops 20%. What do you tell them?
**Listen first, then reconnect them to their plan with facts, and only change course if their goals or situation have changed.**

1. **Acknowledge the fear:** "It's completely normal to feel this way. A 20% drop is painful."
2. **Revisit the plan:** their time horizon and goals, why the portfolio was built this way, and confirm their near-term spending is covered by cash and bonds, so they won't be forced to sell stocks at low prices.
3. **Give perspective:** drops of 20% or more have happened many times, including in 2008, 2020 and 2022, and the market eventually recovered from each one, sometimes quickly. Selling locks in the loss and risks missing the rebound, because many of the best days come close to the worst ones.
4. **Offer useful actions:** rebalancing, which means buying stocks at lower prices; harvesting tax losses; or, if their tolerance for risk really is lower than they thought, a gradual adjustment rather than a panicked one.
5. **Follow up:** check in regularly until things settle.

The interviewer is testing empathy, calm and your grasp of investor behavior.

**Follow-up:** What if the client says they can't sleep and wants to sell everything today?

## What's 17 × 24?

## What's 15% of 360?

## A stock rises 50% and then falls 50%. Where does it end up?
**Down 25%.** $100 rises 50% to $150, then falls 50% to $75. The order doesn't matter: 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75. The general lesson: recovering from a loss takes a bigger percentage gain than the loss itself (a 50% loss needs a 100% gain), which is why big drawdowns do so much damage.

**Follow-up:** How much does a stock need to rise to recover from a 20% drop?

## A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much is the ball?
**5 cents.** If the ball costs x, the bat costs x + $1.00, so 2x + $1.00 = $1.10, and x = $0.05. The instinctive answer, 10 cents, is wrong: the bat would then cost $1.10, and the total would be $1.20. The question tests whether you check your first instinct.

**Follow-up:** If 5 machines take 5 minutes to make 5 widgets, how long do 100 machines take to make 100 widgets?

## What is the probability of rolling at least one six in two rolls of a die?
**11/36, or about 30.6%.** The easy way is to find the chance of no sixes and subtract it from 1. No six on one roll is 5/6, so no six on two rolls is 5/6 × 5/6 = 25/36, and at least one six is 1 − 25/36 = 11/36. Adding 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3 is wrong because it counts the double six twice.

**Follow-up:** What about at least one six in three rolls?

## What is the sum of the whole numbers from 1 to 100?
**5,050.** Pair the numbers from both ends: 1 + 100, 2 + 99, 3 + 98 and so on. Each pair adds up to 101, and there are 50 pairs, so 50 × 101 = 5,050. The general formula is n(n + 1) ÷ 2.

**Follow-up:** What's the sum of the odd numbers from 1 to 99?

## What is the angle between the hands of a clock at 3:15?
**7.5 degrees.** The minute hand points at the 3, which is 90 degrees from 12. The hour hand moves 30 degrees an hour, or half a degree a minute, so at 3:15 it's 15 × 0.5 = 7.5 degrees past the 3, at 97.5 degrees. The gap is 97.5 − 90 = 7.5 degrees. The trap is answering zero.

**Follow-up:** What's the angle at 9:45?

## How many gas stations are there in the United States? Walk me through your estimate.
**Show a clear structure, use round numbers and sanity-check the result.** One approach, from demand:

- About 330 million people and roughly 280 million vehicles.
- Each vehicle fills up about once a week: about 280 million fill-ups a week, or about 40 million a day.
- A typical station has about 8 pumps, each handling about 30 fill-ups a day (busy at rush hour, quiet at night): about 250 fill-ups a station a day.
- 40 million ÷ 250 = **about 160,000 stations.**

Sanity check: that's roughly one station for every 2,000 people, so a town of 10,000 would have about five, which sounds right. For reference, the National Association of Convenience Stores counts about 120,000 convenience stores that sell fuel, and they sell most of the country's gasoline, so a total in the 140,000 to 160,000 range is about right. Interviewers care far more about the logic than the final number.

**Follow-up:** How would your estimate change as electric cars become more common?

## Estimate the annual revenue of a coffee shop near your campus.
**Break it into customers × average spend × days open, and say your assumptions out loud.**

- Open about 360 days a year, roughly 6 a.m. to 8 p.m.
- Customers a day: a morning rush of about 60 an hour for three hours (180), plus about 20 an hour for the other 11 hours (220), so about 400.
- Average ticket: about $7, a latte plus the occasional pastry.
- Daily revenue: 400 × $7 = $2,800.
- Annual revenue: $2,800 × 360 ≈ **$1 million.**

Then adjust: a campus shop is slower in summer and over breaks, so cut maybe 20%, to around $800,000. Mention how you'd check it in real life: count customers during a rush, look at the menu prices and think about seasons.

**Follow-up:** How would you estimate its profit?

## You have a 3-gallon jug and a 5-gallon jug. How do you measure exactly 4 gallons?
1. Fill the 5-gallon jug.
2. Pour from it into the 3-gallon jug until the 3 is full. That leaves 2 gallons in the 5.
3. Empty the 3-gallon jug and pour the 2 gallons into it.
4. Fill the 5-gallon jug again.
5. Pour from the 5 into the 3, which already holds 2 gallons, so only 1 gallon fits. That leaves exactly **4 gallons** in the 5-gallon jug.

Another route: fill the 3 and pour it into the 5, then fill the 3 again and top up the 5, leaving 1 gallon in the 3. Empty the 5, pour in that 1 gallon, then add a full 3. Talk through each step out loud.

**Follow-up:** How would you measure exactly 1 gallon?

## Roughly what is 1.07 to the 10th power? Explain your shortcut.
**About 1.97, so roughly 2.** Shortcut: the rule of 72. At 7%, money doubles in about 72 ÷ 7, a little over 10 years, so after 10 years it's just under double. A more precise route: 1.07² ≈ 1.145, so 1.07⁴ ≈ 1.311 and 1.07⁸ ≈ 1.718, and 1.718 × 1.145 ≈ 1.967.

**Follow-up:** How long does money take to triple at 10% a year?

## How many times a day do the hour and minute hands of a clock overlap?
**22 times.** In 12 hours, the minute hand goes around 12 times and the hour hand once, so the minute hand laps the hour hand 12 − 1 = 11 times. Over 24 hours, that's 22. The trap is answering 24: there's no separate overlap between 11 and 12, because the one near 11 actually happens at 12:00. The hands overlap about every 65.45 minutes, which is 12/11 of an hour.

**Follow-up:** How many times a day do the hands form a right angle?
