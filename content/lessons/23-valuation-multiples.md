# Valuation multiples: P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue, and which ones go together
*Comes up in:* Every valuation discussion, from banking technicals to "pitch me a stock" in asset management.

## Cold open
"Why do you pair enterprise value with EBITDA but equity value with net income?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
A multiple divides a value by a measure of performance, so you can compare companies of different sizes, like comparing houses by price per square foot. The one rule: the top and the bottom have to belong to the same investors. Enterprise value belongs to everyone who funds the company, so it goes with measures calculated before interest is paid: revenue, EBITDA and EBIT. Equity value belongs to shareholders, so it goes with measures after interest: net income and earnings per share.

## How it works
The common multiples:

| Multiple | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| P/E (price ÷ earnings per share) | Mature, profitable companies | Distorted by debt, taxes and one-time items; useless when earnings are negative |
| EV/EBITDA | Most industries; comparing companies with different debt | Ignores capex |
| EV/EBIT | Capital-heavy businesses, since it counts depreciation | Still ignores working capital |
| EV/Revenue | Young or unprofitable companies | Ignores profitability entirely |
| Price/book | Banks and insurers | Book values can be stale |

A quick example: a stock at $40 with earnings per share of $2 trades at 20 times earnings. A company with an EV of $1.2 billion and EBITDA of $150 million trades at 8 times EBITDA.

Why does mismatching break? EV divided by net income compares the value to all investors with a profit that has already had the lenders' interest taken out. The numerator and denominator are measuring different things.

What makes one company deserve a higher multiple than another: faster growth, higher and more stable margins, better returns on capital and lower risk.

## In the real world
Multiples are how the market expresses its mood. By FactSet's count, the S&P 500 has averaged about 19 times the next year's expected earnings over the past ten years. It ran above 20 for much of 2024 and 2025 as investors paid up for the largest technology companies, then drifted back to about 19 by September 2026. A high multiple isn't automatically a bubble: it can reflect faster expected growth. It does mean the market has less room for disappointment. Look up the S&P 500's current forward P/E in FactSet's weekly Earnings Insight report before an asset management interview.

## The interview trap
The traps are using P/E on a company with negative earnings, and pairing EV with net income or equity value with EBITDA.

> A multiple should compare a value and a metric that belong to the same group of investors. Enterprise value represents everyone who funds the company, debt holders and shareholders, so it goes with metrics calculated before interest is paid, like revenue, EBITDA or EBIT. Equity value belongs only to shareholders, so it goes with metrics after interest, like net income or earnings per share, which gives you the P/E ratio. Mixing them, like dividing enterprise value by net income, compares the value to all investors with a profit that has already had the lenders' share taken out. EV to EBITDA is also popular because it isn't distorted by differences in debt and taxes, so you can compare companies with very different capital structures.

## Your line
"Match the numerator and the denominator to the same investors." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Why might two companies in the same industry trade at very different EV/EBITDA multiples?" **The other side:** Multiples are shortcuts that hide assumptions about growth and risk; a DCF forces you to state them.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Company A trades at 10 times EBITDA and Company B at 15 times. What could explain the gap?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. A stock is at $30 with earnings per share of $1.50. What's its P/E?
   b. Which multiple would you use for a company with negative earnings but growing revenue?
   c. Why is price-to-book common for banks?
3. **Rewind:** What goes into the EV bridge?
4. **Brain teaser:** A company's P/E is 20. What's its earnings yield?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Company B may be growing faster, have higher or steadier margins, earn better returns on capital, carry less risk or hold a stronger market position. Its EBITDA might also be temporarily depressed, or the market might expect a takeover. A strong answer names several and says how you'd check.
- **a.** 20 times.
- **b.** EV/Revenue, or EV/gross profit.
- **c.** A bank's assets and liabilities are mostly financial and carried near market value, and debt is part of its operations, so enterprise value doesn't work well; book value and return on equity are more meaningful.
- **Rewind:** Equity value, plus debt, preferred stock and noncontrolling interests, minus cash and non-operating assets.
- **Brain teaser:** 5%. Earnings yield is the inverse of P/E: 1 ÷ 20.
