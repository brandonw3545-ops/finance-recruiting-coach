# Your "why finance" story: an answer only you could give
*Comes up in:* Every first round and most club interviews, usually alongside "why this seat" and "why our firm."

## Cold open
"Why finance?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
"I like money" and "I like fast-paced environments" are answers anyone could give, so they tell the interviewer nothing. A strong answer is specific to you. It has a **spark** (a real moment that got you interested), a **follow-through** (what you did about it, which proves the interest is real) and a **fit** (why the actual work of the seat suits you). Think of it as a stock pitch where you're the stock: a thesis, the evidence, and why it matters for this buyer.

## How it works
Build each part from your own life.

- **Spark:** a concrete moment. A family business, an investment that went wrong, a class that clicked, a job where you saw money decisions up close.
- **Follow-through:** proof. A club role, a pitch you built, a course you finished, investing your own money with a written process, reading earnings releases every quarter.
- **Fit:** the work, not the lifestyle. Analysis that leads to a decision, explaining it to people, working on deals in a team, building long-term client relationships.

Here's how weak answers turn into strong ones.

| Weak | Why it's weak | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "I've always loved finance." | No evidence | "My first stock fell 30% after an earnings report I hadn't read, and I wanted to understand what I'd missed." |
| "It's fast-paced and challenging." | True of many jobs | "I like problems where the numbers settle the argument, like defending my club's pitch." |
| "I want to make a lot of money." | Turns interviewers off | Leave it out. Talk about growth and the work. |

## In the real world
The best origin stories come with a lesson, not just a moment. Warren Buffett bought his first stock at age 11: three shares of Cities Service preferred at about $38. It fell to around $27, he sold when it got back to $40, and then watched it climb to roughly $200. He has told that story for decades because of what it taught him about patience and not anchoring to the price you paid. Your story doesn't need to be famous; it needs a moment, a lesson and what you did next. Keep a short note on why you made each investment decision so you have real examples to draw on.

## The interview trap
The trap is going generic or going long. A two-minute biography loses the interviewer, and a vague answer sounds like everyone else's.

> My interest started in high school, when I bought my first stock, a company I used every day, and it dropped 25% after an earnings report I hadn't even read. I wanted to understand what I'd missed, so I taught myself to read financial statements, joined my school's investment club and started writing a short thesis before every investment I make. What I found is that I like the combination of hard analysis and having to defend a view to other people. That's what draws me to asset management: you build a view from the numbers, and then you have to convince a portfolio manager with it.

## Your line
"The best why-finance answers have a moment, a follow-through and a fit." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Why not consulting?" or "What would you do if this didn't work out?" **The other side:** Some interviewers care less about your story and more about whether you understand the job. Make sure the fit part describes the work accurately.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Answer "Why finance?" in 60 seconds with a spark, a follow-through and a fit.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Why is "I like fast-paced environments" a weak answer?
   b. What's one way to prove your interest besides saying it?
   c. How should "why finance" differ from "why this seat"?
3. **Rewind:** What are the three beats of a strong "tell me about yourself"?
4. **Brain teaser:** What is 17 × 24?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer names a specific moment, shows concrete actions you took afterward, connects to the real work of the seat, avoids money and empty adjectives, and lands in about 60 seconds.
- **a.** It's true of many jobs, so it says nothing about finance or about you.
- **b.** Point to action: a club role, a pitch you built, a course you completed, or a habit such as reading earnings releases.
- **c.** "Why finance" explains your interest in the field; "why this seat" shows you understand that specific job and why it suits you better than the alternatives.
- **Rewind:** A hook (who you are and what sparked your interest), proof (two or three experiences with results) and why this seat now.
- **Brain teaser:** 408. 17 × 25 = 425, then subtract one 17.
