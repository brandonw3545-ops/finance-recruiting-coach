# The three financial statements: what each one tells you
*Comes up in:* Nearly every technical first round, as "Walk me through the three financial statements."

## Cold open
"Walk me through the three financial statements."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Every public company reports three statements, and each answers a different question. The **income statement** shows performance over a period: revenue minus expenses equals net income. The **balance sheet** is a snapshot at one moment: what the company owns (assets) and how it paid for them (liabilities and equity). The **cash flow statement** shows the cash that actually came in and went out over the period. Think of your own money: the income statement is your pay minus your bills for the year, the balance sheet is what you own and owe today, and the cash flow statement is what actually happened in your bank account.

## How it works
Here's a simple company, Coffee Co., for one year.

| Income statement | | Cash flow statement | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,000 | Net income | 150 |
| Cost of goods sold | (400) | + Depreciation | 50 |
| Operating expenses | (300) | − Increase in working capital | (30) |
| Depreciation | (50) | Cash flow from operations | 170 |
| Operating income | 250 | Capital expenditures | (100) |
| Interest | (50) | Debt repaid and dividends | (30) |
| Taxes at 25% | (50) | **Net change in cash** | **40** |
| **Net income** | **150** | | |

The balance sheet at year end lists assets (cash, receivables, inventory, property and equipment), liabilities (payables, debt) and shareholders' equity (money shareholders put in plus profits kept over time). Assets always equal liabilities plus equity.

What each one is best for: the income statement shows profitability; the balance sheet shows financial health, like how much debt there is and whether the company can pay its bills; the cash flow statement shows whether profits turn into real cash, which makes it the hardest statement to dress up.

## In the real world
Amazon is the classic example of why you need all three. For years it reported thin profits, yet its operating cash flow ran far ahead of its net income, because it collects cash from customers within days but pays its suppliers weeks later. An investor who read only the income statement missed that the business was funding its own growth. The reverse also happens: a company can report rising profits while its cash flow shrinks, which is often an early warning. Next earnings season, pick a company you follow and compare its net income with its cash flow from operations.

## The interview trap
The trap is describing each statement but never saying how they connect, or saying the cash flow statement shows "profit." It shows cash.

> The income statement shows a company's revenue, expenses and profit over a period, ending in net income. The balance sheet is a snapshot at a point in time of the company's assets, liabilities and shareholders' equity, and assets always equal liabilities plus equity. The cash flow statement starts with net income, adjusts for non-cash items like depreciation and for changes in working capital to get cash flow from operations, then adds cash flow from investing, like capital expenditures, and from financing, like issuing debt or paying dividends, to show the net change in cash. They link together because net income flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet and to the top of the cash flow statement, and the ending cash on the cash flow statement becomes the cash balance on the balance sheet.

## Your line
"Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact." **Follow-up you'll get:** "If you could only look at one statement, which would you pick?" **The other side:** Cash flow can be managed too. Paying suppliers later makes operating cash flow look better for a while, so check whether payables are ballooning.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** If you could use only one financial statement to judge a company, which would you pick and why?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Which statement is a snapshot at a single point in time?
   b. What are the three sections of the cash flow statement?
   c. Where does net income show up on the balance sheet?
3. **Rewind:** In one sentence, what's the difference between the sell side and the buy side?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's 0.6 divided by 0.08?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Most strong answers pick the cash flow statement, because it shows whether the company really generates cash and is the hardest to manipulate, then note you'd still want the balance sheet to check debt. Picking the balance sheet for a bank or another asset-heavy business is also defensible if you explain why.
- **a.** The balance sheet.
- **b.** Operating activities, investing activities and financing activities.
- **c.** In retained earnings, inside shareholders' equity: net income adds to it and dividends reduce it.
- **Rewind:** The sell side gets paid for transactions and services; the buy side gets paid for managing money and how it performs.
- **Brain teaser:** 7.5. Multiply both by 100: 60 divided by 8.
