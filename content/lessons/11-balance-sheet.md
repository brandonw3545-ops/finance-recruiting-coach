# The balance sheet: why assets always equal liabilities plus equity
*Comes up in:* Accounting technicals, and any interview about a bank, an insurer or a company with heavy debt.

## Cold open
"Why does the balance sheet always balance?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Everything a company owns had to be paid for somehow: either with borrowed money (liabilities) or with owners' money (equity, including the profits the company has kept). So assets equal liabilities plus equity by definition. Accounting is double-entry, which means every transaction touches at least two accounts in a way that keeps the two sides equal. Think of a house worth $500,000 with a $400,000 mortgage: the house is the asset, the mortgage is the liability, and your $100,000 of equity is the difference.

## How it works
The structure runs from most liquid to least.

- **Assets.** Current: cash, accounts receivable (money customers owe), inventory, prepaid expenses. Long-term: property, plant and equipment (PP&E), intangible assets, goodwill.
- **Liabilities.** Current: accounts payable (money owed to suppliers), accrued expenses, deferred revenue, short-term debt. Long-term: long-term debt, lease obligations.
- **Equity.** The money shareholders invested, plus retained earnings (all past net income minus dividends), minus any shares the company has bought back.

Watch both sides move with each transaction:

| Transaction | Assets | Liabilities | Equity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Borrow $100 | Cash +100 | Debt +100 | |
| Buy $40 of inventory with cash | Cash −40, inventory +40 | | |
| Sell that inventory for $60 cash, ignoring taxes | Cash +60, inventory −40 | | Retained earnings +20 |
| Issue $50 of new stock | Cash +50 | | Common stock +50 |

Every row balances. In the third row, assets rise by 20 and equity rises by 20, because the $20 profit belongs to the owners.

## In the real world
McDonald's has reported negative shareholders' equity for years. That isn't a sign of a failing business: it borrowed heavily to buy back shares and pay dividends, which shrank equity below zero, while its restaurants kept producing strong cash flow. The balance sheet still balances, because liabilities are larger than assets. The lesson is that book equity is an accounting record of what was invested and kept, not what a company is worth. To see this for yourself, open a 10-K, find total liabilities and shareholders' equity, and check what has been driving equity up or down.

## The interview trap
The trap is saying "because accountants make it balance," or not knowing what retained earnings is.

> The balance sheet balances because every asset a company has must have been paid for somehow: either by borrowing, which is a liability, or by its owners, which is equity. Equity includes the money shareholders put in plus the profits the company has kept over time, called retained earnings. Accounting is double-entry, so every transaction hits at least two accounts in a way that keeps both sides equal. For example, if a company borrows $100, cash goes up by $100 on the asset side and debt goes up by $100 on the liability side. If it then earns a $20 profit in cash, cash goes up by $20 and retained earnings goes up by $20.

## Your line
"Every asset was paid for by someone: a lender or an owner." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Can shareholders' equity be negative? What would that mean?" **The other side:** Many assets sit on the balance sheet at historical cost, so book value can be far from what the assets are worth today.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** A company borrows $100 and uses it to buy equipment. Walk me through what happens to the balance sheet.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Name two current assets and two current liabilities.
   b. What is retained earnings?
   c. A company has $500 of assets and $350 of liabilities. What's its equity?
3. **Rewind:** What's the difference between gross profit and operating income?
4. **Brain teaser:** You have a 3-gallon jug and a 5-gallon jug. How do you measure exactly 4 gallons?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Borrowing raises cash by $100 and debt by $100. Buying the equipment lowers cash by $100 and raises PP&E by $100. The net result is PP&E up $100 and debt up $100, so the balance sheet balances, with no effect on the income statement until depreciation starts.
- **a.** Current assets: cash, accounts receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses. Current liabilities: accounts payable, accrued expenses, deferred revenue, short-term debt.
- **b.** All the net income a company has earned over its life, minus the dividends it has paid.
- **c.** $150.
- **Rewind:** Gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold; operating income also subtracts operating expenses like SG&A, R&D and depreciation.
- **Brain teaser:** Fill the 5-gallon jug and pour it into the 3-gallon jug, leaving 2 gallons. Empty the 3, pour the 2 gallons into it, fill the 5 again, and top off the 3 (which takes 1 gallon). Exactly 4 gallons are left in the 5-gallon jug.
