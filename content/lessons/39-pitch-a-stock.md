# How to pitch a stock: thesis, catalysts, valuation, risks
*Comes up in:* Asset management, equity research, hedge fund and investment club interviews, and sometimes as a curveball in banking.

## Cold open
"Pitch me a stock."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
A stock pitch is an argument, not a company profile. The interviewer wants to know what the market is getting wrong, why that will change, what the stock is worth and what could prove you wrong. Most students stumble by describing the business ("Apple makes iPhones") instead of making a case. Think of a lawyer's closing argument, not an encyclopedia entry.

## How it works
Structure a two-minute pitch in five parts:

1. **Recommendation:** buy or sell, today's price, your price target and the time frame.
2. **Thesis:** two or three reasons the market is mispricing the stock, ideally a "variant view": something you believe that most investors don't.
3. **Catalysts:** specific events that will make the market see what you see, like an earnings report, a product launch, a cost-cutting plan or a spinoff.
4. **Valuation:** how you got your target, usually a multiple applied to next year's numbers, checked against peers or a DCF.
5. **Risks:** what would make you wrong, and the signal that would make you change your mind.

Here's the structure applied to an imaginary gym chain, Harbor Fitness:

| Part | Example |
|---|---|
| Recommendation | Buy at $40, target $52.50 in 12 months: about 31% upside |
| Thesis | The market fears new gyms will steal members from existing ones, but new locations are breaking even within 18 months, and a recent price increase stuck without members leaving |
| Catalysts | Next quarter's results, when new-location numbers become visible; an investor day in the spring |
| Valuation | Next year's earnings of $3.50 a share × 15, the low end of peers' 15 to 17 times, = $52.50; it trades at about 11 times today |
| Risks | A recession cuts memberships; a low-cost rival opens nearby. I'd sell if existing gyms lost members two quarters in a row |

A few tips:
- Pick a company you can really defend, ideally not the most famous giants, unless you have a genuinely unusual angle. A mid-sized company in an industry you know is ideal.
- Know the numbers cold: share price, market value, P/E or EV/EBITDA, revenue growth, and how they compare with peers.
- Prepare a one-sentence version, a two-minute version and enough depth for 20 minutes of questions.
- Have a stock to sell ready too; some interviewers ask for one.
- Update it the week of the interview. If the stock has moved 20%, they'll ask what changed.

## In the real world
In December 2012, the hedge fund manager Bill Ackman presented a 342-slide case that Herbalife, a seller of nutrition shakes and supplements, was a pyramid scheme, and bet about $1 billion against its stock. Carl Icahn took the other side and bought shares. In 2016, the Federal Trade Commission made Herbalife pay $200 million and change how it paid its distributors, but stopped short of calling it a pyramid scheme, and the stock rose. Ackman closed out his bet in 2018 with a large loss. The research was deep; what the thesis lacked was a catalyst that actually arrived. Ask the same question of your own pitch: what event makes the market agree with you, and when?

## The interview trap
The traps are describing the company instead of arguing a case, skipping valuation or risks, and saying a stock is "undervalued" without explaining why the market is wrong. Here's the shape of a strong answer, using the imaginary company above; in an interview, use a real one you know well.

> I'd buy Harbor Fitness, a mid-sized gym chain trading at $40, with a 12-month target of about $52, roughly 30% upside. The market prices it at about 11 times next year's earnings, below peers at 15 to 17 times, because it fears new locations will steal members from existing ones. I think that's wrong for two reasons: new gyms are breaking even within 18 months, and a recent price increase has stuck without members leaving. The catalyst is next quarter's report, when results for the newer locations become visible. At 15 times next year's earnings of $3.50 a share, the stock is worth about $52. The main risks are a recession hitting memberships and a low-cost competitor; I'd change my mind if existing gyms lost members for two quarters in a row.

## Your line
"A pitch answers one question: what does the market have wrong, and how will it find out?" **Follow-up you'll get:** "What's the bear case, and why are you right and the sellers wrong?" **The other side:** Many great investors don't rely on near-term catalysts at all; they buy excellent businesses at fair prices and wait years for the value to show.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Give a 30-second thesis for a company whose product you use every week.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Next year's earnings per share are $4 and your target multiple is 18 times. What's your price target?
   b. What's a catalyst?
   c. The stock trades at $60 and your target is $72. What's the upside?
3. **Rewind:** Why do most active managers trail the index over long periods?
4. **Brain teaser:** A stock falls 20% and then rises 25%. Where does it end up?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer opens with a recommendation, gives one or two specific reasons the market is wrong, names a catalyst, and mentions the main risk, all in about 30 seconds. It sounds like an argument, not a product review.
- **a.** $72.
- **b.** A specific, foreseeable event, like an earnings report or product launch, that should make the market recognize what your thesis says.
- **c.** 20%.
- **Rewind:** Before costs, active managers as a group earn the market's return; fees and trading costs then pull the average below the index.
- **Brain teaser:** Back where it started: 0.8 × 1.25 = 1.
