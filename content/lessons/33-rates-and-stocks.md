# Why interest rates move stock prices
*Comes up in:* Sales and trading, asset management and equity research interviews, especially "what's going on in the markets" questions.

## Cold open
"Why do stocks often fall when interest rates rise?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Higher interest rates pull stock prices down through three channels. First, **valuation**: rates are the base of every discount rate, so when they rise, future profits are worth less today. Second, **competition**: when safe Treasuries pay 5%, stocks have to offer a higher expected return to be worth the risk, and the only way an existing stock's expected return rises is for its price to fall. Third, **the economy**: higher borrowing costs slow spending and raise companies' interest bills, which cuts earnings. Think of interest rates as gravity for asset prices: the stronger the pull, the lower prices sit.

## How it works
The valuation channel hits hardest when a company's profits lie far in the future. Here's the simple formula for a company growing steadily forever: value = next year's cash flow ÷ (discount rate − growth rate).

| | Steady company | Growth company |
|---|---|---|
| Cash flow next year | 100 | 100 |
| Growth rate | 1% | 4% |
| Value at an 8% discount rate | 1,429 | 2,500 |
| Value at a 9% discount rate | 1,250 | 2,000 |
| **Change** | **−12.5%** | **−20%** |

A one-point rise in the discount rate cuts the growth company's value by much more, because more of its value comes from cash flows far in the future. It behaves like a long-duration bond.

The competition channel shows up in the **earnings yield**, the inverse of the P/E ratio. A market at 20 times earnings has an earnings yield of 5%. When 10-year Treasuries yielded 1.5%, as they did at the start of 2022, that 5% looked generous; with Treasuries at 4.5%, the extra return for taking stock market risk is thin.

Not every stock reacts the same way. Banks can earn more when rates rise, because they can charge more on loans than they pay on deposits, at least for a while. Utilities and real estate stocks, owned for their steady dividends, behave like bonds and tend to fall. Unprofitable growth companies usually get hit hardest.

And the reason rates are moving matters. Rates rising because the economy is strong can go hand in hand with rising earnings and stock prices. Rates rising because of inflation fears usually hurt.

## In the real world
On December 18, 2024, the Fed cut interest rates by a quarter point, and stocks fell anyway: the S&P 500 dropped about 3% and the Dow lost more than 1,100 points. Officials had signaled fewer cuts in 2025 than investors had hoped for, and markets trade on the expected path of rates, not on any single decision. In 2022, the reverse logic played out over a whole year: as the Fed raised rates far faster than expected, the S&P 500 fell about 19% and the tech-heavy Nasdaq about 33%. ## The interview trap
The traps are claiming that higher rates always mean lower stocks, and ignoring the fact that markets react to changes in expectations, not to rates alone.

> Higher rates hurt stock prices through three channels. First, valuation: interest rates are the base of the discount rate, so when they rise, future profits are worth less today, and companies whose profits are far in the future, like high-growth tech, get hit hardest. Second, competition: when safe Treasuries pay more, investors demand a higher expected return from stocks, and prices fall until they offer it. Third, the economy: higher borrowing costs slow consumer spending and business investment and raise companies' interest expense, which cuts into earnings. But it depends on why rates are moving and what was expected. If rates rise because growth is strong, stocks can still do fine, and markets react to the expected path of rates more than to any single decision.

## Your line
"Interest rates are gravity for asset prices: the higher they go, the less future cash is worth today." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Why did growth stocks fall so much more than value stocks in 2022?" **The other side:** In 2023 and 2024, stocks rallied even with rates at their highest in years, because earnings grew and inflation cooled, which shows rates are only one input.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Why do growth stocks usually fall more than value stocks when rates rise?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. A company's cash flow next year is 50, it grows 3% a year forever, and the discount rate is 8%. What's it worth?
   b. Name a sector that can benefit from higher interest rates.
   c. A stock index trades at 25 times earnings. What's its earnings yield?
3. **Rewind:** Who votes on the FOMC?
4. **Brain teaser:** A company's value falls from 2,500 to 2,000. What's the percentage drop?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** More of a growth company's value comes from cash flows far in the future, and the further out a cash flow is, the more a higher discount rate shrinks its present value. A strong answer compares growth stocks to long-term bonds and mentions that unprofitable companies may also need to raise money at higher cost.
- **a.** 1,000: 50 ÷ (0.08 − 0.03).
- **b.** Banks, and often insurers.
- **c.** 4%: 1 ÷ 25.
- **Rewind:** The seven Fed governors, the president of the New York Fed, and four of the other eleven regional Fed presidents on a rotating basis: 12 in all.
- **Brain teaser:** 20%.
