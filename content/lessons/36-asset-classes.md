# Asset classes: stocks, bonds, cash, real estate and alternatives
*Comes up in:* Asset management and wealth management interviews, and "how would you invest $10,000?" questions anywhere.

## Cold open
"Walk me through the main asset classes. What role does each one play in a portfolio?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
An asset class is a group of investments that behave in similar ways. The main ones are stocks, bonds, cash, real estate and alternatives, and each has a job. Stocks grow wealth over the long run. Bonds provide income and usually cushion a portfolio when stocks fall. Cash keeps money safe and available. Real estate provides income and some protection against inflation. Alternatives try to earn returns that don't move with the stock market. Choosing among them always means trading off return, risk and liquidity, which is how quickly you can sell without taking a loss.

## How it works
Here's how the three classic U.S. asset classes have done since 1928, using NYU professor Aswath Damodaran's data through 2024:

| Asset class | Main job | Average annual return since 1928 | Worst calendar year | Liquidity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stocks (S&P 500) | Growth | About 10% | −44% (1931) | High |
| Bonds (10-year Treasuries) | Income and stability | About 4.5% | −18% (2022) | High |
| Cash (3-month Treasury bills) | Safety and liquidity | About 3.3% | Never negative | Highest |

Inflation averaged about 3% a year over that period, so cash has barely kept up, while stocks have grown real wealth, with frightening drops along the way.

The other two:

- **Real estate:** rental income plus price gains, with some protection against inflation. Owning property directly is illiquid; real estate investment trusts (REITs) trade like stocks.
- **Alternatives:** private equity, hedge funds, private credit, infrastructure and commodities. They aim for returns that don't move with public markets, usually with higher fees and much less liquidity. A private equity fund typically locks up investors' money for ten years or more.

## In the real world
2022 broke a rule many investors took for granted. Inflation drove interest rates sharply higher, which hurt stocks and bonds at the same time: the S&P 500 lost about 18% including dividends, U.S. investment-grade bonds lost about 13%, and a classic mix of 60% stocks and 40% bonds lost about 16%, its worst year since 2008. Commodities, meanwhile, rose. The lesson: bonds cushion stocks when the problem is weak growth, but when the problem is inflation, they can fall together. And cash, often dismissed as dead money, paid about 5% in 2023. To see how professionals mix asset classes for someone your age, look up the holdings of a target-date retirement fund dated around 2070.

## The interview trap
The traps are listing asset classes without saying what job each one does, assuming bonds always rise when stocks fall, and ignoring liquidity.

> The main asset classes are stocks, bonds, cash, real estate and alternatives, and each plays a different role. Stocks are for long-term growth: historically they've returned around 10% a year in the U.S., but with big drops along the way. Bonds provide income and usually cushion a portfolio when stocks fall, though they can fall together when inflation pushes rates up, as in 2022. Cash is for safety and liquidity, with low returns. Real estate offers income and some inflation protection but is less liquid, unless you own it through REITs. Alternatives, like private equity, hedge funds, private credit and commodities, aim for returns that don't move with public markets, usually in exchange for higher fees and less liquidity. How you mix them depends on the investor's goals, time horizon and tolerance for risk.

## Your line
"Every asset class has a job; building a portfolio is deciding how much of each job you need." **Follow-up you'll get:** "How would you invest $10,000 for someone who needs it in one year, compared with someone who needs it in 40?" **The other side:** Critics argue private assets only look steady because they aren't priced every day, a problem AQR's Cliff Asness calls "volatility laundering," so their diversification benefit may be overstated.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** How would you split money between asset classes for a 25-year-old saving for retirement, and why?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Which is least liquid: Treasury bonds, a private equity fund or an S&P 500 index fund?
   b. What does REIT stand for?
   c. Why did stocks and bonds fall together in 2022?
3. **Rewind:** How do asset managers make money?
4. **Brain teaser:** In a portfolio of 60% stocks and 40% bonds, stocks return 10% and bonds 4%. What does the portfolio return?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer leans heavily toward stocks, often 80% to 100%, because a 25-year-old has decades to ride out downturns and needs growth, with a small cash cushion for emergencies and possibly some bonds. It explains the reasoning, time horizon and risk tolerance, rather than just naming percentages, and mentions low-cost diversified funds.
- **a.** The private equity fund.
- **b.** Real estate investment trust.
- **c.** Inflation pushed interest rates sharply higher, which lowered the value of both: future profits were discounted more heavily, and existing bonds fell as new ones paid more.
- **Rewind:** Mostly through a management fee charged as a percentage of assets under management, sometimes plus performance fees.
- **Brain teaser:** 7.6%: 0.6 × 10% + 0.4 × 4%.
