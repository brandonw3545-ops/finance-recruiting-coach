# Inflation: CPI vs. PCE, and why the Fed targets 2%
*Comes up in:* Markets questions in sales and trading, asset management and wealth management interviews, and "what's going on in the economy" small talk anywhere.

## Cold open
"What's the difference between CPI and PCE, and which one does the Fed target?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Inflation is the rise in the general level of prices, so each dollar buys less. There are two main gauges. The **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, tracks a basket of goods and services that urban consumers pay for out of pocket. The **PCE price index** (personal consumption expenditures), published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis, covers a wider range of spending, including things paid for on households' behalf, like employer-provided health insurance. The Fed targets 2% annual inflation as measured by PCE. Think of CPI as your own receipts, and PCE as the receipts for everything spent on you, including what your employer and the government pay.

## How it works
| | CPI | PCE price index |
|---|---|---|
| Published by | Bureau of Labor Statistics | Bureau of Economic Analysis |
| Covers | What urban consumers pay out of pocket | All consumer spending, including what's paid on households' behalf |
| Weights | Updated once a year | Shift as people switch between products |
| Housing's share | About a third | About a sixth |
| Usually runs | A bit higher | A bit lower |
| Used for | Social Security raises, inflation-protected Treasuries (TIPS), tax brackets | The Fed's 2% target |

**Headline vs. core:** core inflation leaves out food and energy, whose prices jump around. The Fed's target is headline PCE, but it watches core PCE as a better guide to where inflation is heading.

**Nominal vs. real:** a real return is roughly the nominal return minus inflation. A savings account paying 4% when inflation is 3% earns a real return of about 1%.

**Why 2%, and not zero?**
1. A little inflation leaves room to cut interest rates in a downturn. With zero inflation, rates would sit closer to zero, with less room to cut.
2. Deflation, falling prices, is dangerous: people put off purchases, and debts get heavier in real terms.
3. Price indexes tend to overstate inflation slightly, because they don't fully capture quality improvements.
4. A stable, well-known target anchors expectations, so businesses and workers set prices and wages around it.

The Fed formally adopted the 2% target in January 2012. In 2020, it said it would let inflation run moderately above 2% after periods below it, to make up the difference; in August 2025, it dropped that make-up approach but kept the 2% PCE target.

## In the real world
Inflation surged after the pandemic, driven by snarled supply chains, stimulus-fueled demand and an energy price spike after Russia invaded Ukraine. CPI inflation hit 9.1% in June 2022, its highest since November 1981. The Fed had called the rise "transitory" in 2021; by the time inflation peaked, it was in the middle of its fastest run of rate increases in four decades. The episode is why interviewers still ask about inflation, and why "is inflation back to 2%?" remains a live question. The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases CPI around the middle of each month; know the latest headline and core numbers, and which way they're heading.

## The interview trap
The traps are saying the Fed targets CPI, saying the Fed wants zero inflation, and thinking that falling inflation means falling prices. It doesn't: inflation dropping from 6% to 3% means prices are still rising, just more slowly.

> Both measure how fast consumer prices are rising. CPI, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, tracks a basket of goods and services that urban consumers pay for out of pocket. PCE, from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, is broader: it includes spending made on households' behalf, like employer-paid health insurance, and it adjusts its weights as people switch between products, so it usually runs a little below CPI. The Fed targets 2% inflation as measured by PCE, and it watches core PCE, which excludes food and energy, as a guide to the underlying trend. It aims for 2% rather than zero because a little inflation gives it room to cut rates in a downturn and protects against deflation.

## Your line
"The Fed's target is 2% on PCE: not CPI, and not zero." **Follow-up you'll get:** "If inflation falls from 6% to 3%, are prices falling?" **The other side:** Some economists argue for a higher target, like 3%, to give the Fed more room to cut rates in a recession; others say changing the target would damage the Fed's credibility.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Why doesn't the Fed aim for zero inflation?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. A basket of goods cost $200 last year and $210 this year. What's the inflation rate?
   b. Your savings account pays 4% and inflation is 3%. What's your real return?
   c. What does core inflation leave out?
3. **Rewind:** Why does a 30-year bond lose more value than a 2-year bond when rates rise?
4. **Brain teaser:** At 3% inflation, roughly how many years until prices double?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A little inflation gives the Fed room to cut rates in a downturn, guards against deflation, which makes people delay spending and makes debts heavier, and offsets the slight overstatement in price indexes. A strong answer adds that a stable, known target anchors expectations.
- **a.** 5%.
- **b.** About 1%.
- **c.** Food and energy.
- **Rewind:** More of its cash flows are far in the future, so a change in the discount rate affects their present value much more. It has a longer duration.
- **Brain teaser:** About 24 years: 72 ÷ 3.
