# The income statement, line by line
*Comes up in:* Accounting technicals in every seat, and any time you discuss a company's earnings.

## Cold open
"Walk me through the income statement."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
The income statement starts with revenue (the top line) and subtracts costs in layers, showing profit at each level until it reaches net income (the bottom line). Each layer answers a question. Is the product profitable? Is the business profitable? What's left after paying lenders? What's left after taxes? Think of peeling an onion: you start with every dollar of sales and peel away costs until you reach what belongs to shareholders.

## How it works
Here's Coffee Co. for one year.

| Line | Amount | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,000 | Sales earned in the period, not cash collected |
| − Cost of goods sold | (400) | The direct cost of what was sold |
| **= Gross profit** | **600** | A 60% gross margin: how profitable the product is |
| − Selling, general and administrative | (250) | Salaries, rent, marketing |
| − Research and development | (50) | Spending on new products |
| − Depreciation and amortization | (50) | The cost of long-lived assets, spread over time |
| **= Operating income (EBIT)** | **250** | A 25% operating margin: the core business |
| − Interest expense | (50) | The cost of debt |
| **= Pre-tax income** | **200** | |
| − Taxes at 25% | (50) | |
| **= Net income** | **150** | A 15% net margin: what belongs to shareholders |

Earnings per share (EPS) is net income divided by shares outstanding: with 100 shares, Coffee Co. earns $1.50 a share. EBITDA, which you'll hear constantly, is operating income plus depreciation and amortization: 300 here.

Two rules matter. The statement is prepared on an **accrual** basis: revenue is recorded when it's earned and expenses when they're incurred, regardless of when cash moves. And companies often present depreciation inside cost of goods sold or operating expenses rather than on its own line, so read the notes.

## In the real world
Margins tell you what kind of business you're looking at. Costco marks up its goods so little that its gross margin is only about 11%. The real profit engine is membership fees: about $4.6 billion in fiscal 2023, equal to most of the company's net income of about $6.3 billion. Software companies are the opposite, often with gross margins above 70%, because selling one more copy costs almost nothing. In the next earnings release you read, find the gross margin and operating margin and compare them with a year earlier.

## The interview trap
The trap is listing line items without saying what each level means, or mixing up the order of interest and taxes.

> The income statement shows a company's profitability over a period. It starts with revenue, then subtracts cost of goods sold to get gross profit, which shows how profitable the product itself is. Then you subtract operating expenses like SG&A, R&D and depreciation to get operating income, or EBIT, the profit from the core business. Then you subtract interest expense to get pre-tax income, and taxes to get net income, the bottom line that belongs to shareholders. It's prepared on an accrual basis, so revenue is recorded when it's earned, not when the cash comes in, and expenses are matched to the period they relate to.

## Your line
"Each level of the income statement answers a question: is the product profitable, is the business profitable, and what's left after lenders and taxes?" **Follow-up you'll get:** "Why can a company with rising revenue have falling net income?" **The other side:** Net income can be distorted by one-time items, so analysts often look at operating income or adjusted figures, and then check what was adjusted.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Why can a company with growing revenue still have shrinking profits?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What's the difference between gross profit and operating income?
   b. Coffee Co. has revenue of 1,000 and operating income of 250. What's its operating margin?
   c. What is EPS?
3. **Rewind:** In one sentence each, what do the three financial statements show?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's 7% of 250?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Costs can grow faster than revenue: lower gross margins from discounts or higher input costs, rising SG&A or R&D, more depreciation from new investment, higher interest expense or taxes, or one-time charges. A strong answer names where on the statement each one hits.
- **a.** Gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold; operating income also subtracts operating expenses such as SG&A, R&D and depreciation.
- **b.** 25%.
- **c.** Earnings per share: net income divided by shares outstanding.
- **Rewind:** The income statement shows performance over a period, the balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time, and the cash flow statement shows the actual cash in and out.
- **Brain teaser:** 17.5. One percent of 250 is 2.5, and 7 × 2.5 = 17.5.
