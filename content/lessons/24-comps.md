# Comparable companies analysis ("comps")
*Comes up in:* Banking and equity research technicals, and any conversation about how a company or an IPO gets priced.

## Cold open
"Walk me through a comparable companies analysis."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Comps value a company by looking at how the market values similar public companies. You find a group of peers, calculate their valuation multiples, and apply those multiples to your company. Think of pricing a house by looking at what similar houses on the same street sold for per square foot, then adjusting for the extra bedroom.

## How it works
Five steps:

1. **Pick the peer group.** Same industry, but also similar size, growth, margins, business model and geography.
2. **Gather the data.** Share prices, diluted shares, debt and cash for each peer, to get equity value and enterprise value; then revenue, EBITDA and earnings, both for the last twelve months and for next year's consensus estimates.
3. **Calculate the multiples,** such as EV/Revenue, EV/EBITDA and P/E.
4. **Look at the range.** Focus on the median and the middle of the range rather than the average, which outliers distort.
5. **Apply them** to your company's own numbers to get a range of implied values.

An example. Five peers trade at 8x, 9x, 10x, 11x and 12x EBITDA.

| Step | Low | Median | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| EV/EBITDA multiple | 9x | 10x | 11x |
| × Target's EBITDA of $50 million | | | |
| = Implied enterprise value | $450M | $500M | $550M |
| − Net debt of $100 million | $350M | $400M | $450M |
| ÷ 20 million shares = value per share | $17.50 | $20.00 | $22.50 |

The strength of comps is that they're based on real market prices and are quick to build. The weaknesses: no two companies are identical, the peer group drives the answer, and if the whole sector is overvalued, so is your result.

## In the real world
IPOs are priced largely off comps. When Arm, the chip designer, went public in September 2023 at $51 a share, valuing it at about $54.5 billion, much of the debate was about which peers it resembled. Investors who compared it with fast-growing chip and software companies thought the multiple was reasonable; those who compared it with slower-growing chip designers thought it was expensive. Same company, different peer group, different answer. When a company files to go public, its prospectus and the analyst coverage that follows show which peers the bankers chose.

## The interview trap
The traps are choosing peers by industry alone, averaging multiples that include wild outliers, and mixing last year's numbers with next year's.

> In a comps analysis, you value a company based on how the market values similar public companies. First, you pick a peer group: companies in the same industry with similar size, growth, margins and geography. Then you calculate each peer's equity value and enterprise value and divide by metrics like revenue, EBITDA and earnings, for both the last twelve months and next year's estimates. You look at the range of those multiples, focusing on the median and the middle of the range, and apply them to your company's own metrics to get a range of implied enterprise values, which you convert to equity value and a price per share. It's quick and reflects current market sentiment, but it's only as good as the peer group, and it inherits whatever the market is getting wrong.

## Your line
"Comps tell you what the market will pay today, not what a business is truly worth." **Follow-up you'll get:** "How would you pick peers if there's no perfect comparable?" **The other side:** For pricing a deal in the near term, comps are often more useful than a DCF, because they're anchored in real trades.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** How would you choose a peer group for a company with no direct public competitors?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Peers trade at 8x to 12x EBITDA, with a median of 10x. The target's EBITDA is $40 million. What's the implied EV at the median?
   b. Why use the median instead of the average?
   c. Name one advantage and one drawback of comps.
3. **Rewind:** Why do you pair enterprise value with EBITDA?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's 15 × 16?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Widen the search to companies with a similar business model, customers, end markets, growth and margins, even in adjacent industries; weight the closest ones most; and cross-check with private deals in the space. A strong answer admits the trade-off between a bigger peer group and a more similar one.
- **a.** $400 million.
- **b.** A few outliers can pull the average far from what's typical; the median isn't affected by them.
- **c.** Advantage: it's based on real market prices and quick to build. Drawback: the answer depends heavily on the peer group, and it inherits any mispricing in the market.
- **Rewind:** Both belong to all capital providers: EV includes debt and equity, and EBITDA is calculated before interest.
- **Brain teaser:** 240.
