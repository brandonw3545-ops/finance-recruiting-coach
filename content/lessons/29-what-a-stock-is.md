# What a stock actually is, and how the stock market works
*Comes up in:* Sales and trading, asset management and equity research first rounds, and as a warm-up anywhere.

## Cold open
"What actually happens when you buy a share of stock, and why do stock prices move?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
A share of stock is a small slice of ownership in a company: a claim on its profits after everyone else, from lenders to suppliers to the tax authorities, has been paid, usually with a vote on big decisions. A company sells shares to raise money, first in an initial public offering (IPO). After that, almost all trading is between investors. When you buy a share of Apple on your phone, Apple doesn't get your money; whoever sold you the share does. The price is set by buyers and sellers, and it reflects what they expect the company to earn in the future. Think of a betting line on a game: it moves with new information, and it already reflects what everyone expects.

## How it works
- **Primary market:** the company sells new shares, in an IPO or a later offering, and receives the money.
- **Secondary market:** investors trade existing shares with each other on exchanges such as the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq. Market makers stand ready to buy and sell all day and earn the small gap between the two prices.
- **Market order vs. limit order:** a market order buys or sells right away at the best available price; a limit order trades only at your price or better.

| Term | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Bid | The highest price a buyer is offering | $49.98 |
| Ask | The lowest price a seller will accept | $50.02 |
| Spread | The gap between them | $0.04 |
| Market cap | Share price × shares outstanding | $50 × 200 million = $10 billion |

What moves prices is a change in expectations, not results alone. If investors expect a company to earn $2 a share and it earns $2, the stock may not move at all; that result was "priced in." If it earns $2 but says next year will be weaker than hoped, the stock can fall on a record quarter. Interest rates and the overall market mood move prices too.

You'll hear about three indexes. The **S&P 500** tracks 500 large U.S. companies, weighted by market value, and is the usual benchmark for "the market." The **Dow Jones Industrial Average** tracks 30 large companies, weighted by share price, an older and quirkier method. The **Nasdaq Composite** includes nearly everything listed on Nasdaq and leans heavily toward technology.

## In the real world
On May 24, 2023, Nvidia forecast about $11 billion of revenue for its next quarter, roughly 50% more than analysts expected, thanks to demand for its artificial intelligence chips. The next day the stock jumped about 24%, adding roughly $184 billion of market value in a single session. Two lessons. Nvidia didn't receive a cent of that money; the gains went to whoever owned the shares. And what moved the price wasn't the quarter it had just reported but the jump in what investors expected next. This earnings season, pick a company that reports, compare its results and forecast with what analysts expected, and see whether the stock's move makes more sense against the expectations than against the raw numbers.

## The interview trap
The traps are saying a stock rose "because the company did well," and thinking the company receives money every time its shares trade.

> A share of stock is a small piece of ownership in a company: a claim on its future profits after its lenders and other obligations are paid, usually with a vote on major decisions. Companies sell shares to raise money, first in an IPO, but after that almost all trading happens between investors on exchanges like the NYSE and Nasdaq, so the company doesn't receive anything when shares change hands. The price is set by supply and demand, and it reflects what investors expect the company to earn in the future, discounted back to today. That's why prices move on changes in expectations rather than results alone: a company can report record profits and still fall if investors expected more.

## Your line
"A stock price isn't a grade on last quarter; it's a bet on everything after it." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Why might a stock fall after a strong earnings report?" **The other side:** In the short run, prices also move on fear, momentum and money flowing in and out of funds; as the investor Benjamin Graham put it, the market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Explain why a company's stock can fall on the day it reports record profits.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. A stock trades at $25 with 400 million shares outstanding. What's its market cap?
   b. The bid is $19.95 and the ask is $20.05. What's the spread?
   c. When you buy a share on the Nasdaq, does the company get your money?
3. **Rewind:** In one breath, walk me through a DCF.
4. **Brain teaser:** A stock falls 50% and then rises 50%. Where does it end up compared with where it started?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** The price already reflected high expectations. If the results or, more often, the forecast for next quarter fall short of what investors expected, the price drops even though profits hit a record. A strong answer uses the words "priced in" and explains that the market reacts to the future, not the past.
- **a.** $10 billion.
- **b.** $0.10.
- **c.** No. You're buying from another investor. The company only receives money when it sells new shares, as in an IPO or a later offering.
- **Rewind:** Project unlevered free cash flow, add a terminal value, discount both at WACC to get enterprise value, subtract net debt to get equity value, and divide by diluted shares.
- **Brain teaser:** Down 25%: 0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75.
