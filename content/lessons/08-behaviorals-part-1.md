# Behaviorals, part 1: strengths, weaknesses and "tell me about a failure"
*Comes up in:* Most first rounds and superdays. "Greatest weakness" and "tell me about a failure" are among the most common questions in any interview.

## Cold open
"What's your greatest weakness?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
These questions test self-awareness and honesty, not perfection. A good weakness is real, relevant but not fatal to the job, and paired with what you're doing about it. A good failure story has a real miss, clear ownership and a change in how you work. Think of it as writing a scouting report on yourself: an honest view of what you do well and what you're working on.

## How it works
**Strengths:** pick two or three the job actually needs, such as attention to detail, work ethic, clear communication or analytical ability, and back each one with a short example.

**Weakness formula:** name it, give an example of when it showed up, explain what you're doing about it, and show evidence it's improving. Avoid fatal weaknesses ("I'm not great with numbers" in a banking interview) and fake ones ("I work too hard," "I'm a perfectionist").

| Weakness | Why it works | How to show progress |
|---|---|---|
| Taking on too much myself instead of delegating | Real and common in strong students | "Now I set owners and a mid-point check-in" |
| Speaking up in large groups | Real, and fixable | "I prepare one point before every meeting" |
| Saying yes to too many commitments | Real | "I keep a weekly priority list and say no earlier" |

**Failure formula:** use STAR, let the result be honestly negative, then add the lesson and what you did differently the next time. A failure where nothing actually went wrong is a humblebrag, and interviewers notice.

## In the real world
Great investors talk openly about their mistakes. Warren Buffett has called buying Berkshire Hathaway itself, then a struggling textile company, one of his biggest mistakes; in a 2010 CNBC interview he estimated it had cost him about $200 billion compared with putting the money into a better business. He writes about errors in his annual letters too. The lesson for an interview: owning a real mistake and explaining what changed sounds stronger than claiming you've never made one. Reading a few years of Berkshire's shareholder letters is also good preparation for asset management interviews.

## The interview trap
The trap is the fake weakness or the failure where nothing went wrong. Both tell the interviewer you either lack self-awareness or won't be straight with them.

> I tend to take on too much myself instead of delegating. Last year, leading a group project for my investment club, I rewrote parts of other people's sections the night before because I didn't trust they'd be done, and I ended up exhausted while the team felt sidelined. Since then I set clear owners at the start and a check-in halfway through, and I give feedback early instead of redoing work. On our last pitch, I didn't touch anyone else's section and we finished a day early. It's still something I watch, but I've gotten much better at it.

## Your line
"The point of a weakness answer is to show I notice my own patterns and fix them." **Follow-up you'll get:** "How would that weakness show up in this job?" **The other side:** Some interviewers prefer a skill gap, like Excel speed, over a personality trait, because training fixes it quickly.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Answer "Tell me about a time you failed" in 60 to 90 seconds, including what you'd do differently.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Why is "I'm a perfectionist" a weak answer?
   b. What are the four parts of a good weakness answer?
   c. Name one weakness you should never give in a banking interview.
3. **Rewind:** What does each letter in STAR stand for?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's the angle between the hands of a clock at 3:15?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer describes a real failure with stakes, takes clear ownership with no blaming, says what you learned, and gives a later example of acting differently, in a calm tone.
- **a.** Interviewers have heard it constantly, and it dodges the question instead of answering it.
- **b.** Name it, give an example, explain what you're doing about it, and show evidence of progress.
- **c.** Anything fatal to the job, such as poor attention to detail, discomfort with numbers or missing deadlines.
- **Rewind:** Situation, Task, Action, Result.
- **Brain teaser:** 7.5 degrees. At 3:15 the minute hand points at the 3 (90 degrees); the hour hand has moved a quarter of the way from 3 to 4, which is another 7.5 degrees, putting it at 97.5 degrees.
