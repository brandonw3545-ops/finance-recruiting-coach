# Asset management vs. wealth management: what each does, and how each makes money
*Comes up in:* Every asset management and wealth management interview, and "why this division?" questions at big banks.

## Cold open
"What's the difference between asset management and wealth management?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
**Asset managers** build and run investment portfolios, such as mutual funds, ETFs, hedge funds and accounts for pension funds, for many clients at once. Their job is the portfolio: beat a benchmark, or track it cheaply. **Wealth managers** work with individuals and families. Their job is the client: understanding their goals and building an overall plan across investments, taxes, retirement and estate planning, often using asset managers' funds as building blocks. Think of an asset manager as a company that makes running shoes, and a wealth manager as a coach who works out which shoes, training plan and diet fit one particular runner.

## How it works
| | Asset management | Wealth management |
|---|---|---|
| Clients | Institutions, like pension funds, endowments, insurers and sovereign wealth funds, plus individuals through funds | Individuals and families, often wealthy ones |
| Focus | The portfolio: performance against a benchmark | The client: goals, taxes, retirement, estate, borrowing |
| How it's paid | A management fee as a percentage of assets under management (AUM), sometimes plus a share of profits | An advisory fee, often around 1% of assets a year, plus commissions, lending and banking fees |
| Typical roles | Research analyst, portfolio manager, trader, sales | Financial advisor, private banker, wealth planner, investment analyst |
| Examples | BlackRock, Vanguard, Fidelity, Capital Group, Wellington, PIMCO | Morgan Stanley Wealth Management, Merrill, J.P. Morgan Private Bank, UBS, Goldman Sachs |

Fees vary widely. An index fund can charge as little as 0.03% of assets a year; an actively managed stock fund often charges 0.5% to 1%; hedge funds historically charged "2 and 20," meaning 2% of assets plus 20% of profits, though fees have come down. The math: a $1 billion fund charging 0.5% earns $5 million a year. An advisor whose clients have $200 million with them at 1% brings in $2 million a year in revenue.

Why both businesses like fees on assets: the revenue recurs, and it grows as markets rise and new money comes in, unlike trading and deal fees, which swing with the markets. The flip side is that revenue falls when markets do.

## In the real world
Morgan Stanley remade itself after the 2008 crisis by leaning into wealth management. Under James Gorman, CEO from 2010 through 2023, it bought out Citigroup's share of the Smith Barney brokerage, completing the purchase in 2013, then bought the online broker E*Trade in 2020 and the asset manager Eaton Vance in 2021. The bet was that fees on client assets would be far steadier than trading and deal revenue. Goldman Sachs has since reorganized around a similar push into asset and wealth management. Pick a bank you're interviewing with, open its latest quarterly earnings release, and find how much of its revenue comes from wealth and asset management.

## The interview trap
The traps are treating the two as the same, describing asset management as "managing money for rich people" (that's closer to wealth management), and not knowing how either one gets paid.

> Asset management is about running portfolios: firms like BlackRock or Fidelity manage funds and accounts for pension funds, endowments, insurers and individuals investing through mutual funds and ETFs, and their job is to beat or track a benchmark. They mostly earn a fee based on assets under management. Wealth management is about the client: advisors work with individuals and families on their whole financial life, including how their money is invested, taxes, retirement and estate planning, often using asset managers' funds as building blocks. They typically earn an advisory fee, often around 1% of assets a year, plus commissions and lending income. So the asset manager builds the products, and the wealth manager builds the client's plan.

## Your line
"Asset managers build the products; wealth managers build the plan, often out of those products." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Which would you rather do, and why?" **The other side:** The line is blurring: many big firms run both businesses, and wealth managers increasingly build their own model portfolios instead of simply picking funds.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Why might someone prefer a career in asset management over wealth management, or the other way around?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. A $2 billion fund charges 0.75% a year. What's its annual fee revenue?
   b. What does AUM stand for?
   c. Name one way a wealth manager gets paid other than a percentage of assets.
3. **Rewind:** What does an inverted yield curve mean?
4. **Brain teaser:** What does a 1% annual fee cost on a $750,000 portfolio?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer connects each path to the work itself. Asset management suits someone who loves analyzing companies and markets and wants to be judged on investment results; wealth management suits someone who loves working with people, building long relationships and solving whole financial problems, and it rewards building a client base. The best answers tie the choice to something specific from the candidate's own experience.
- **a.** $15 million.
- **b.** Assets under management.
- **c.** Any of: commissions on trades or products, interest on loans such as mortgages or loans against a portfolio, banking fees, or flat fees for financial planning.
- **Rewind:** Short-term Treasury yields are above long-term ones. Markets expect rates to fall, usually because growth is expected to slow, and it has historically come before most recessions.
- **Brain teaser:** $7,500 a year.
