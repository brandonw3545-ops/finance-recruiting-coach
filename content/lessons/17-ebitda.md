# EBITDA: why bankers love it and why Buffett and Munger didn't
*Comes up in:* Banking, private equity and credit interviews, and any valuation question that uses EV/EBITDA.

## Cold open
"Why is EBITDA so widely used, and what are its flaws?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: roughly operating income plus D&A. Bankers love it because it strips out how a company is financed (interest), where it pays taxes, and how it depreciates its assets, so it's an easy way to compare the core operations of different companies. Its critics point out that it also strips out real costs. Think of judging a restaurant by its sales minus food and staff costs while ignoring that the kitchen needs replacing every few years.

## How it works
For Coffee Co., operating income is 250 and D&A is 50, so EBITDA is 300. It's used for three big things: valuation multiples (enterprise value divided by EBITDA), leverage measures (debt divided by EBITDA, which lenders use to size and limit loans), and quick comparisons across companies.

The problem is what it leaves out. Two companies can have identical EBITDA and very different cash:

| | Software company | Steel mill |
|---|---|---|
| EBITDA | 300 | 300 |
| Capital expenditures | (20) | (200) |
| **EBITDA minus capex** | **280** | **100** |

EBITDA ignores capex, changes in working capital, taxes and interest, all of which are real cash. And because it isn't defined by accounting rules, companies report "adjusted EBITDA" that adds back whatever they consider one-time, which needs checking.

## In the real world
Warren Buffett put the objection memorably in his 2000 letter to Berkshire shareholders: references to EBITDA make us shudder, he wrote, asking whether management thinks the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures. Charlie Munger was blunter still. Adjustments can go too far: in 2018, WeWork sold bonds using a measure it called "community-adjusted EBITDA," which left out costs such as marketing and general and administrative expenses. Critics mocked it, and the company's 2019 IPO collapsed. In any earnings release, compare adjusted EBITDA with GAAP operating income and read the list of what was added back.

## The interview trap
The trap is calling EBITDA "cash flow." It's a rough proxy for operating cash flow before capex, and only that.

> EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, roughly operating income plus D&A. People use it because it removes the effects of capital structure, tax rates and depreciation policy, so it's a quick way to compare the core operating performance of different companies, and it's the basis for multiples like EV to EBITDA and leverage measures like debt to EBITDA. The flaws are that it ignores real cash costs: capital expenditures, working capital needs, taxes and interest. A capital-intensive business can have a lot of EBITDA and very little free cash flow, which is Buffett's point when he asked whether management thinks the tooth fairy pays for capex. And because it isn't a GAAP measure, companies can adjust it aggressively, so you always check what's been added back.

## Your line
"EBITDA is a fine place to start and a terrible place to stop." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Which is better for valuation, EBITDA or free cash flow?" **The other side:** For lenders and buyout firms, EBITDA remains the standard, because it approximates the cash available to pay debt before capex, and it's comparable across deals.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Two companies both have $300 of EBITDA. Why might one be worth far more than the other?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Operating income is 250 and D&A is 50. What's EBITDA?
   b. Name two real costs EBITDA ignores.
   c. How do lenders commonly use EBITDA?
3. **Rewind:** In one sentence, what is working capital?
4. **Brain teaser:** If you double a number and then increase the result by 50%, what's the total percentage increase?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Differences in capex needs, working capital, growth, the stability of the business, risk, debt and taxes, which all show up in free cash flow but not EBITDA. A strong answer gives a concrete contrast, like an asset-light software company versus a steel mill.
- **a.** 300.
- **b.** Any two of capital expenditures, working capital, taxes and interest.
- **c.** To size loans and set limits, such as a maximum ratio of debt to EBITDA in loan agreements.
- **Rewind:** Current assets minus current liabilities; for operations, receivables plus inventory minus payables.
- **Brain teaser:** 200%. Doubling and then multiplying by 1.5 triples the number.
