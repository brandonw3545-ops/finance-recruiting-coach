# DCF, part 2: the discount rate (WACC, CAPM and beta)
*Comes up in:* Banking and corporate finance technicals, and any question about what rising interest rates do to valuations.

## Cold open
"How do you calculate WACC, and why is the cost of equity usually higher than the cost of debt?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
The discount rate is the return investors require for the risk of owning the business. The **weighted average cost of capital (WACC)** blends what shareholders require and what lenders require, weighted by how much of each the company uses. Think of a blended interest rate when you pay for a house partly with a cheap mortgage and partly with a pricier loan from a relative who's taking more risk.

## How it works
The formula:

**WACC** = (equity share × cost of equity) + (debt share × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate))

The **cost of equity** usually comes from the capital asset pricing model (CAPM):

**Cost of equity** = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium

- The **risk-free rate** is usually the 10-year Treasury yield.
- **Beta** measures how much a stock moves with the market: 1.0 moves with it, above 1.0 swings more.
- The **equity risk premium** is the extra return investors demand for owning stocks instead of Treasuries, often estimated at 4% to 6%.

Here's an example:

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Risk-free rate | 4% |
| Beta | 1.2 |
| Equity risk premium | 5% |
| **Cost of equity** = 4% + 1.2 × 5% | **10%** |
| Pre-tax cost of debt | 6% |
| **After-tax cost of debt** = 6% × (1 − 25%) | **4.5%** |
| Weights | 70% equity, 30% debt |
| **WACC** = 70% × 10% + 30% × 4.5% | **8.35%** |

Equity costs more than debt because shareholders are paid last and have no guaranteed return, and interest is tax-deductible, which makes debt cheaper still. But more debt doesn't keep lowering WACC: past a point, both lenders and shareholders see more risk and demand more.

## In the real world
The risk-free rate moved more in 2022 than in any recent year. The 10-year Treasury yield started the year at about 1.5% and ended near 3.9%, and it touched 5% in October 2023. Every DCF's starting point shifted with it: higher risk-free rates mean higher costs of equity and debt, a higher WACC and lower present values, especially for companies whose cash flows sit far in the future. That's a big reason growth stocks fell so hard in 2022. Before any valuation question, know roughly where the 10-year yield is today.

## The interview trap
The traps are forgetting the tax shield on debt, using book values for the weights instead of market values, and claiming that adding debt always lowers WACC.

> WACC is the weighted average of what each type of investor requires: the cost of equity times equity's share of total capital, plus the after-tax cost of debt times debt's share. The cost of equity usually comes from CAPM: the risk-free rate, typically the 10-year Treasury yield, plus beta times the equity risk premium, where beta measures how much the stock moves with the market. The cost of debt is what the company pays to borrow, multiplied by one minus the tax rate, because interest is tax-deductible. Equity costs more than debt because shareholders are paid last and have no guaranteed return, so they demand more. That's also why adding debt lowers WACC only up to a point: past that, lenders and shareholders both see more risk and raise their required returns.

## Your line
"WACC is the hurdle a company has to clear to create value for everyone who funds it." **Follow-up you'll get:** "What happens to a DCF valuation if interest rates rise?" **The other side:** CAPM is a simplification: beta is noisy and the equity risk premium is debated, so practitioners cross-check with other methods.

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A higher risk-free rate raises the cost of equity and the cost of debt, so WACC rises and future cash flows are worth less today. The terminal value, usually most of the value, falls the most, so companies whose cash flows sit far in the future get hit hardest. A strong answer may add that higher rates can also slow growth.
- **a.** 11.5%: 4% + 1.5 × 5%.
- **b.** 6%: 8% × 0.75.
- **c.** 8%: 0.6 × 10% + 0.4 × 5%.
- **Rewind:** A company is worth the present value of the cash it will generate in the future.
- **Brain teaser:** 0.42.
