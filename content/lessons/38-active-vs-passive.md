# Active vs. passive: the index fund revolution, and why most pros trail the index
*Comes up in:* Nearly every asset management interview, plus wealth management and equity research.

## Cold open
"Do you believe in active or passive investing?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
**Passive** investing means buying the whole market through an index fund and accepting the market's return at very low cost. **Active** investing means picking securities to try to beat a benchmark. The economist William Sharpe laid out the core math in 1991: before costs, the average actively managed dollar must earn exactly the market's return, because all investors together are the market. After fees and trading costs, the average active dollar must trail it. Some managers do beat the market, but they're hard to identify in advance, and few keep doing it. Think of a poker game where the house takes a cut of every pot: the players can't collectively beat the table, and after the house's cut, most of them lose.

## How it works
| | Passive | Active |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Match the index | Beat the index |
| Typical cost | As little as 0.03% a year | Often 0.5% to 1% a year |
| Trading | Only when the index changes | Whenever the manager sees an opportunity |
| Results vs. the index | Almost identical | Can be much better or much worse |

Fees compound. Invest $10,000 for 30 years at 7% a year and you end up with about $76,100. Take 0.6% a year off that return for fees and you end up with about $64,300, roughly $11,800 less, for the fee difference alone.

John Bogle founded Vanguard and launched the first index mutual fund for individual investors in 1976; critics called it "Bogle's folly." By the end of 2023, according to Morningstar, passive U.S. mutual funds and ETFs held more money than active ones for the first time: about $13.3 trillion against $13.2 trillion.

Active management has its best case in less crowded corners of the market, like small companies, emerging markets and corporate bonds, during market dislocations, when forced sellers create bargains, and for investors with specific needs, like managing taxes. And markets need active investors: if everyone simply indexed, nobody would be setting prices.

## In the real world
In 2008, Warren Buffett made a $1 million bet, with the winnings going to charity, that an S&P 500 index fund would beat a selection of hedge funds over ten years. Protégé Partners took the other side with five funds of hedge funds. From 2008 through 2017, the index fund gained about 126%, roughly 8.5% a year. The best of the five gained about 88%, roughly 6.5% a year, and the rest did far worse. The broader data agrees: S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA scorecard found that about 90% of actively managed U.S. large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 over the 15 years through 2024, and 79% trailed it in 2025 alone. If you're interviewing at an active manager, read what that firm has published about where active investing works; most have a view.

## The interview trap
The trap is giving a dogmatic answer, especially "active is dead" in an interview at an active manager. The other is not knowing why most active funds trail.

> I think passive is the right default for most people's core savings, because the math is hard to beat: before costs, active managers as a group earn the market return, so after fees most of them trail it, and SPIVA data shows roughly nine in ten large-cap U.S. funds underperforming the S&P 500 over fifteen years. But I don't think active is dead. Markets need active investors to set prices, and skilled managers have a better shot in less efficient areas, like small companies, emerging markets and credit, or during dislocations when forced sellers create bargains. So I'd look for active managers with a clear, repeatable edge, reasonable fees and a process I can evaluate, and I'd use passive everywhere else.

## Your line
"Passive is the right default; active has to earn its fee, and it can, in the less crowded corners of the market." **Follow-up you'll get:** "If index funds keep growing, what happens to how efficiently markets are priced?" **The other side:** Some argue that as passive investing grows, money flows mechanically into the biggest stocks and prices become less efficient, creating more opportunities for active managers.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** You're interviewing at an active fund manager, and they ask whether passive is better. Answer in 30 seconds.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Who founded Vanguard and launched the first index mutual fund for individual investors?
   b. Why must the average active dollar trail the index after costs?
   c. Name a market where active management has a better chance.
3. **Rewind:** What does correlation measure, and why does it matter for diversification?
4. **Brain teaser:** What does a 0.6% annual fee cost on $1 million?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer respects both sides: it acknowledges the math and the data on fees, then explains where and how skilled active management adds value, ideally tied to that firm's strategy, and ends with a clear view rather than a shrug.
- **a.** John Bogle, in 1976.
- **b.** Before costs, all active investors together hold the market and earn its return; fees and trading costs then pull the average below it.
- **c.** Any of: small companies, emerging markets, corporate or high-yield bonds, or private markets.
- **Rewind:** How closely two investments move together, from −1 to +1. The lower the correlation, the more their ups and downs cancel out, so mixing them reduces risk without reducing expected return.
- **Brain teaser:** $6,000 a year.
