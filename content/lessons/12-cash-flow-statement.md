# The cash flow statement: why profit isn't the same as cash
*Comes up in:* Accounting technicals in every seat, and especially in asset management and credit interviews.

## Cold open
"Why isn't net income the same as cash flow?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
The income statement records revenue when it's earned and expenses when they're incurred, and it includes expenses that never use cash, like depreciation. The cash flow statement tracks the cash itself, in three sections: operating, investing and financing. Think of earning a paycheck that won't land in your account until next month: you earned the money this month, but your bank balance doesn't know that yet.

## How it works
Most companies use the indirect method: start with net income and adjust it to cash. Here's Coffee Co.

| Line | Amount | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | 150 | From the income statement |
| + Depreciation | 50 | An expense that used no cash |
| − Increase in accounts receivable | (40) | Sales booked but not yet collected |
| − Increase in inventory | (20) | Cash spent on goods not yet sold |
| + Increase in accounts payable | 30 | Bills received but not yet paid |
| **= Cash flow from operations** | **170** | |
| − Capital expenditures | (100) | Investing: buying equipment |
| **= Cash flow from investing** | **(100)** | |
| − Debt repaid | (20) | Financing |
| − Dividends paid | (10) | Financing |
| **= Cash flow from financing** | **(30)** | |
| **Net change in cash** | **40** | 170 − 100 − 30 |

The rule of thumb for working capital: when an asset like receivables or inventory goes up, cash goes down; when a liability like payables goes up, cash goes up. Free cash flow, in its simplest form, is cash from operations minus capital expenditures: 70 here.

## In the real world
The textbook warning is W.T. Grant, once one of the largest U.S. retailers. For most of the decade before its 1975 bankruptcy, it reported positive net income while cash flow from operations was negative, as receivables and inventory piled up. A 1980 study of the case by Largay and Stickney became a standard reading on why analysts should look past earnings to cash. Today the same check takes a minute: for a company you follow, compare net income with cash flow from operations over the last few years and see whether they move together.

## The interview trap
The trap is saying depreciation "generates cash." It doesn't: it's added back because it never used cash in the first place. The only real cash effect of depreciation is that it lowers taxes.

> Net income is calculated on an accrual basis, so it includes non-cash items and timing differences. Depreciation reduces net income but doesn't use any cash, so it gets added back. Working capital matters too: if a company sells on credit, it records revenue before collecting the cash, so a rise in accounts receivable reduces cash flow, and buying inventory uses cash before the expense shows up. Some uses of cash, like capital expenditures and debt repayment, don't hit net income at all. The cash flow statement reconciles all of that: it starts with net income, adjusts for non-cash items and working capital to get operating cash flow, then adds investing and financing flows to get the actual change in cash.

## Your line
"A growing company can be profitable and still run out of cash if customers pay slowly and inventory piles up." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Which matters more to you, net income or cash flow from operations?" **The other side:** Operating cash flow can be flattered by stretching payments to suppliers, so check whether payables are growing much faster than sales.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** A company's net income rose 20%, but its cash flow from operations fell. What could explain that?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. In which section of the cash flow statement does capex appear?
   b. Accounts receivable rose by 30. Does that raise or lower cash flow from operations?
   c. Where do dividends show up on the cash flow statement?
3. **Rewind:** Why does the balance sheet always balance?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's the probability of flipping three heads in a row with a fair coin?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Working capital building up (receivables or inventory growing faster than sales, or paying suppliers faster), a gain included in net income that isn't operating cash (like selling an asset, whose cash shows up in investing), lower non-cash add-backs, or a one-time cash payment such as taxes. A strong answer names the specific line.
- **a.** Investing activities, as a negative number.
- **b.** It lowers it by 30, because the revenue was recorded but the cash wasn't collected.
- **c.** In financing activities.
- **Rewind:** Every asset is funded by liabilities or equity, and double-entry accounting keeps both sides equal with every transaction.
- **Brain teaser:** 1/8, or 12.5%: 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2.
