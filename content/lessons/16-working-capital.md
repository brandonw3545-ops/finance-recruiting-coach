# Working capital: why fast-growing companies can run out of cash
*Comes up in:* Accounting technicals, credit and private equity interviews, and any question about why a growing company needs money.

## Cold open
"What is working capital, and how can a growing company run out of cash even when it's profitable?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. The part that matters for running the business is **receivables plus inventory minus payables**: the cash tied up in day-to-day operations. To grow, a company usually has to buy more inventory and give customers time to pay before the cash comes in. If customers pay slowly and suppliers want cash quickly, growth eats cash. Think of a lemonade stand that has to buy lemons today but won't get paid by its biggest customer until next month.

## How it works
Say a company's sales grow from 1,000 to 1,500. It keeps receivables at 20% of sales, inventory at 15% and payables at 10%.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Change | Cash effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 1,000 | 1,500 | | |
| Accounts receivable | 200 | 300 | +100 | −100 |
| Inventory | 150 | 225 | +75 | −75 |
| Accounts payable | 100 | 150 | +50 | +50 |
| **Net working capital** | **250** | **375** | **+125** | **−125** |

If year-two net income is 100 and depreciation is 20, cash flow from operations is 100 + 20 − 125 = −5. The company is profitable and still burning cash, and the faster it grows, the bigger the gap.

The **cash conversion cycle** measures how long cash is tied up: days sales outstanding (how long customers take to pay) plus days inventory outstanding, minus days payables outstanding (how long the company takes to pay suppliers). A shorter cycle means growth needs less cash.

## In the real world
Amazon runs the opposite model, with a negative cash conversion cycle: it collects from customers within days but pays many suppliers weeks later. Every extra dollar of sales brings cash in before the bills come due, so its growth helped fund itself. Subscription businesses can work the same way, because customers pay up front. The danger runs the other way for a company whose receivables grow faster than its revenue: customers are paying more slowly, and that cash has to come from somewhere. In the next earnings report you read, compare the growth in receivables with the growth in revenue.

## The interview trap
The trap is defining working capital correctly but never explaining the cash mechanism, or saying working capital "is cash."

> Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. For operations, the part that matters is receivables plus inventory minus payables: the cash tied up in running the business. When a company grows, it usually has to build inventory and give customers credit before it gets paid, while suppliers expect to be paid on their own schedule, so more sales means more cash tied up. Net income can be positive, but if the increase in working capital is bigger than net income plus non-cash expenses, operating cash flow turns negative. If the company can't fund that gap with debt or equity, it can run out of cash while still profitable on paper. Businesses with negative working capital, like Amazon, are the opposite: customers pay before suppliers do, so growth actually brings in cash.

## Your line
"Growth costs cash before it makes cash, unless your customers pay you first." **Follow-up you'll get:** "How would you fix a working capital problem?" **The other side:** Stretching supplier payments improves cash flow, but pushed too far it strains relationships, and some companies use supply chain financing that can make cash flow look stronger than it is.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** How can a company improve its operating cash flow without increasing sales?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What's the formula for the cash conversion cycle?
   b. Receivables rise by $50 and payables rise by $20. What's the net effect on operating cash flow?
   c. Why do subscription businesses often have negative working capital?
3. **Rewind:** Depreciation goes up by $10 with a 25% tax rate. What happens to cash?
4. **Brain teaser:** Estimate the annual revenue of a coffee shop near your campus.

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Collect receivables faster, hold less inventory, negotiate longer payment terms with suppliers, cut costs, or sell assets it doesn't need. A strong answer mentions the trade-offs, like losing customers by tightening credit or straining suppliers.
- **a.** Days sales outstanding plus days inventory outstanding minus days payables outstanding.
- **b.** It falls by $30.
- **c.** Customers pay up front, which creates deferred revenue, before the company has delivered the service or paid many of its costs.
- **Rewind:** Cash goes up by $2.50, the tax saved.
- **Brain teaser:** One approach: about 300 customers a day at an average of $6 is $1,800 a day; times about 360 days is roughly $650,000 a year. A strong answer states its assumptions (hours, busy and slow periods, ticket size, summer break) and sanity-checks the result.
