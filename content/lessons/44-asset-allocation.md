# Asset allocation: the 60/40 portfolio, strategic vs. tactical, and glide paths
*Comes up in:* Wealth management and asset management interviews, and "how would you invest $1 million?"

## Cold open
"What is a 60/40 portfolio, and is it still a good idea?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
**Asset allocation** is deciding how much of a portfolio goes into each asset class: stocks, bonds, cash and everything else. It comes before picking any single investment, and it does most of the work. Research going back to a well-known 1986 study by Brinson, Hood and Beebower found that a fund's long-run mix explained the large majority of how its returns moved over time. Picking the mix is the big decision. Picking the stocks is the small one.

## How it works
The classic starting point is **60/40**: 60% stocks for growth and 40% bonds for income and stability. The idea is that when stocks fall, bonds usually hold up or rise, which cushions the drop.

Rough arithmetic: if stocks return 8% and bonds return 4%, a 60/40 mix returns 0.6 × 8% + 0.4 × 4% = 6.4%, with much smaller swings than stocks alone.

Two ways to manage the mix:

- **Strategic allocation** sets long-term targets from the client's goals and risk tolerance, and sticks to them.
- **Tactical allocation** tilts away from those targets for a while, on a view about markets. For example, moving from 60% to 65% stocks because they look cheap.

A **glide path** changes the mix as a date approaches. A target-date retirement fund might hold around 90% stocks when the investor is 25 and shift steadily toward bonds as they near 65. The reasoning is capacity: a young saver has decades to recover from a crash, and a retiree doesn't.

## In the real world
2022 was the year 60/40 failed at its one job. Inflation pushed the Federal Reserve to raise rates fast, and stocks and bonds fell together. The S&P 500 lost about 18% and the main U.S. bond index lost about 13%, its worst year on record, so a 60/40 portfolio was down roughly 16%. Commentators declared it dead. Then yields were higher, which meant bonds finally paid real income again, and the mix recovered. If you manage any portfolio, even a paper one, write down its stock and bond split today and what you'd expect it to lose in a year like 2022.

## The interview trap
The trap is treating 60/40 as a rule for everyone, or saying it's dead without explaining why it struggled.

> A 60/40 portfolio is 60% stocks and 40% bonds. Stocks drive growth, and bonds provide income and usually cushion stock declines. It's a starting point, not an answer: the right mix depends on the client's time horizon, goals and tolerance for loss. It had a bad 2022 because inflation and rate hikes hit stocks and bonds at once, so the cushion failed. But higher yields since then mean bonds pay more income and have more room to rally if stocks fall. I'd still use a stock and bond core for most clients, adjust the split to the person, and consider adding assets that hold up when inflation is the problem.

## Your line
"The mix matters more than the picks." **Follow-up you'll get:** "How would the allocation differ for a 30-year-old and a 65-year-old?" **The other side:** Skeptics say stocks and bonds only moved in opposite directions during a long stretch of low inflation, and that the relationship isn't something to count on.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** How would you invest $1 million for a 40-year-old who won't need the money for 20 years?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What's the difference between strategic and tactical allocation?
   b. What does a glide path do?
   c. Stocks return 10% and bonds 5%. What does a 60/40 mix return?
3. **Rewind:** Why does combining assets with low correlation lower risk?
4. **Brain teaser:** A portfolio is 60% stocks and 40% bonds. Stocks fall 20% and bonds are flat. What percent of the portfolio is in stocks now?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Ask about goals and risk tolerance first. With a 20-year horizon they have the capacity for mostly stocks, something like 70 to 80%, diversified across U.S. and international, with bonds for stability and some cash for emergencies. A strong answer explains the reasoning and mentions rebalancing, not a list of tickers.
- **a.** Strategic sets long-term targets and holds them. Tactical makes temporary tilts based on a market view.
- **b.** It shifts the mix toward safer assets as a target date gets closer.
- **c.** 8%.
- **Rewind:** Their ups and downs partly cancel out, so the mix swings less than its parts.
- **Brain teaser:** About 54.5%.
