# Rebalancing: the discipline of trimming winners
*Comes up in:* Wealth management and asset management interviews, usually as a follow-up to an asset allocation question.

## Cold open
"A client's stocks have had a great year. Why would you sell some?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
**Rebalancing** is bringing a portfolio back to its target mix after markets have pushed it away. Left alone, the winners grow into a bigger share, and the portfolio quietly becomes riskier than the one the client agreed to. Rebalancing sells some of what went up and buys what lagged. It's mainly about controlling risk. Any boost to returns is a bonus, not the point.

## How it works
Start with $100,000 at a 60/40 target:

| | Start | After stocks +30%, bonds flat | After rebalancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stocks | $60,000 | $78,000 | $70,800 |
| Bonds | $40,000 | $40,000 | $47,200 |
| Total | $100,000 | $118,000 | $118,000 |
| Stock share | 60% | 66% | 60% |

To get back to target, the advisor sells $7,200 of stocks and buys bonds. Nothing was lost. The client simply holds the risk they signed up for again.

There are two common triggers:

- **Calendar:** rebalance on a schedule, such as once a year.
- **Threshold:** rebalance whenever an asset class drifts more than a set amount, such as five percentage points, from its target.

Two costs keep advisors from doing it constantly. Selling a winner in a taxable account creates a capital gains tax bill, and every trade has a cost. So good advisors rebalance with new deposits and withdrawals first, and do their selling inside retirement accounts, where there's no tax on the trade.

## In the real world
Between February 19 and March 23, 2020, the S&P 500 fell about 34% as the pandemic hit. A 60/40 portfolio drifted to something like 50/50, and rebalancing meant buying stocks in the scariest week in years. Clients who did it bought near the low, and the index was back at a record by August. The rule did what few people could have done by instinct. That's why it's written down in advance. If you track a portfolio, set a target mix now and decide what drift would make you act.

## The interview trap
The trap is saying you rebalance to boost returns, or forgetting taxes.

> Because their portfolio now carries more risk than they agreed to. If stocks have run, a 60/40 portfolio might be 66/34, which means a bigger loss in the next downturn than the plan assumed. Rebalancing trims the winners and adds to what lagged, which brings risk back to target and builds in a habit of selling high and buying low. I'd be careful about how: use new cash and dividends first, sell inside retirement accounts where there's no tax, and only then realize gains in a taxable account.

## Your line
"Rebalancing is a risk decision that happens to make you sell high." **Follow-up you'll get:** "How often should a portfolio be rebalanced?" **The other side:** Momentum investors point out that winners often keep winning for a while, so trimming them early can cost returns in a long bull market.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Markets are down 25% and a client wants to wait before buying more stocks. What do you say?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What's the main purpose of rebalancing?
   b. Name the two common triggers.
   c. Why rebalance inside a retirement account first?
3. **Rewind:** What's the difference between strategic and tactical asset allocation?
4. **Brain teaser:** A $200,000 portfolio is 70% stocks. How much stock do you sell to get back to 60%?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Remind them of the plan they agreed to when markets were calm, and that their stock share is now below target, so buying is just returning to it. Nobody can pick the bottom, so a strong answer offers to do it in steps if that helps them stay with the plan.
- **a.** Keeping the portfolio's risk at the level the client agreed to.
- **b.** A calendar schedule and a drift threshold.
- **c.** Selling there doesn't trigger capital gains tax.
- **Rewind:** Strategic sets long-term targets and holds them. Tactical makes temporary tilts on a market view.
- **Brain teaser:** $20,000.
