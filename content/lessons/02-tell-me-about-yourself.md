# "Tell me about yourself": turning your resume into a 60-second story
*Comes up in:* Nearly every interview, usually as the first question, and it sets the tone for the next 30 minutes.

## Cold open
"Tell me about yourself."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
This isn't your life story or your resume read aloud. It's a 60-to-90-second argument that you belong in this seat, with a beginning, a middle and an end: what got you into finance, the two or three experiences that prove you're serious, and why this job is the logical next step. Think of it as a movie trailer. A trailer doesn't show every scene; it shows the scenes that make you want to see the movie.

## How it works
Use three beats and give each a time budget.

| Beat | What it covers | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Who you are, plus one line on what sparked your interest in finance | About 10 seconds |
| Proof | Two or three experiences, one or two sentences each, with a result | About 45 seconds |
| Why here | What you want next and why this seat fits | About 15 seconds |

Pick proof points that show what the job needs: curiosity about markets, quantitative ability, work ethic, teamwork and leadership. Here's a fictional example, so you can see the shape:

"I'm a sophomore studying economics. I got into finance through my family's restaurant: in high school I helped with the books and saw how much a slow month hurt our cash, even when we were profitable. Since then I've tried to get closer to markets. I'm on the research team of my school's investment club, where my pitch on a regional bank was voted into our portfolio, and last summer I worked at a local advisory firm preparing review packets for about 40 client households. I also coach youth basketball, which taught me to explain hard things simply. That's why I'm interested in wealth management: it combines the analysis I enjoy with long-term relationships with clients."

What to cut from most first drafts: hometown details, your GPA and classes (they're on the resume), every activity you've ever done, and adjectives with no evidence behind them, like "hardworking" or "passionate."

## In the real world
Timed answers are now normal. Since around 2016, when Goldman Sachs moved many first-round interviews to recorded video, large banks have used recorded interviews that give you a fixed window to respond, often a couple of minutes, with no interviewer nodding along. A tight 60-to-90-second story fits that window and survives a live interviewer who cuts in. To practice for it, record yourself on your phone with a timer, then watch it once. It's painful, and it works.

## The interview trap
The trap is walking chronologically through everything you've done since high school. You run long, the best material gets buried, and the interviewer stops listening.

> I'm a sophomore studying economics, and I got interested in finance through my family's restaurant, where I helped with the books and saw how a slow month could squeeze our cash even when we were profitable. Since then I've tried to get closer to markets. I'm on the research team of my school's investment club, where my pitch on a regional bank was voted into our portfolio, and last summer I worked at a local advisory firm, preparing review packets for about 40 client households. Coaching youth basketball has taught me to explain complicated things simply. That's what draws me to wealth management: it combines the analysis I enjoy with long-term client relationships, and I'd like to start building those skills here.

## Your line
"Every story in my answer earns its place by showing a skill the job needs." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Which of those experiences was the hardest, and why?" **The other side:** Some interviewers want a quick chronological walk through the resume. Read the room, but keep it under 90 seconds either way.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Give your own "tell me about yourself" in under 90 seconds, with a hook, two proof points that each include a number, and a why-this-seat close.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. About how long should your answer be?
   b. Name two things to cut from most first drafts.
   c. What should your last sentence do?
3. **Rewind:** What's the difference between the sell side and the buy side, in one sentence?
4. **Brain teaser:** A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much is the ball?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer runs 60 to 90 seconds; opens with a one-line origin; uses two or three experiences that show finance-relevant skills with concrete results; ends by connecting to this seat; and sounds natural rather than memorized word for word.
- **a.** 60 to 90 seconds, and never more than two minutes.
- **b.** Any two of: hometown or biography details, your GPA and classes, a full list of activities, adjectives with no evidence.
- **c.** Say why this seat (and firm) is your logical next step, which hands the conversation back to the interviewer.
- **Rewind:** The sell side gets paid for transactions and services; the buy side gets paid for managing money and how it performs.
- **Brain teaser:** 5 cents. If the ball is x, the bat is x + $1.00, so 2x + $1.00 = $1.10 and x = $0.05.
