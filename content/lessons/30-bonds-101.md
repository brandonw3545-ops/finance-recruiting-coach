# Bonds 101: coupons, maturities, and why prices and yields move in opposite directions
*Comes up in:* Sales and trading and asset management interviews, any markets question, and banking interviews that touch on debt.

## Cold open
"If interest rates rise, what happens to the price of a bond you already own, and why?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
A bond is a loan you make to a government or a company. In return, it pays you interest, called the coupon, on a set schedule and repays the face value, usually $1,000, on the maturity date. Because those payments are fixed, the price has to do the adjusting when market rates change. If new bonds pay 5%, nobody will pay full price for your old bond paying 4%, so its price falls until a buyer's total return works out to 5%. It's a seesaw: rates up, prices down; rates down, prices up.

## How it works
The key terms:

- **Face value (par):** the amount repaid at maturity, usually $1,000.
- **Coupon rate:** the annual interest as a percentage of face value. A 4% coupon on $1,000 pays $40 a year.
- **Maturity:** when the loan is repaid.
- **Yield to maturity:** the total annual return if you buy at today's price and hold to the end. It's the discount rate that makes the present value of the bond's payments equal its price, the same idea as an IRR.

Take bonds with a 4% coupon, issued at $1,000, paying $40 a year. Here's what happens to their prices if market rates move:

| Maturity | Price if rates rise to 5% | Change | Price if rates fall to 3% | Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 years | $981 | −1.9% | $1,019 | +1.9% |
| 10 years | $923 | −7.7% | $1,085 | +8.5% |
| 30 years | $846 | −15.4% | $1,196 | +19.6% |

Two lessons from the table. First, longer bonds swing much more, because more of their payments sit far in the future. The measure of this sensitivity is **duration**: roughly, a bond with a duration of 8 loses about 8% of its value when rates rise by one percentage point. Second, prices rise more when rates fall than they drop when rates rise by the same amount, a property called **convexity**.

Credit risk is the other big driver. U.S. Treasuries are treated as essentially free of default risk. Companies pay extra yield over Treasuries, called a **credit spread**, depending on how likely they are to pay. Bonds rated BBB− or higher by S&P and Fitch (Baa3 at Moody's) are **investment grade**; anything lower is **high yield**, also called junk.

## In the real world
Silicon Valley Bank put much of a flood of new deposits into long-term Treasuries and mortgage bonds in 2020 and 2021, when rates were near zero. When rates rose in 2022, those bonds lost value. On March 8, 2023, SVB said it had sold about $21 billion of securities at a loss of about $1.8 billion and needed to raise capital. Depositors tried to pull $42 billion the next day, and regulators closed the bank on March 10, at the time the second-largest bank failure in U.S. history. Nothing was wrong with the bonds' credit; they were mostly government-backed. The danger was interest rate risk. Before your next interview, look up today's 2-year and 10-year Treasury yields so you can quote them.

## The interview trap
The traps are saying bond prices fall when rates rise "because the bond gets riskier," and mixing up the coupon rate with the yield.

> Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions because a bond's payments are fixed. If I own a bond with a 4% coupon and market rates rise to 5%, new buyers can get 5% on newly issued bonds, so they'll only buy mine at a discount: low enough that the fixed payments plus the gain back to face value add up to a 5% return. When rates fall, the reverse happens and my bond trades at a premium. The effect is bigger for longer bonds, because more of their cash flows are far in the future. That's what duration measures: a bond with a duration of about 8 loses roughly 8% of its value for a one-point rise in rates.

## Your line
"A bond's payments are fixed, so its price does the adjusting." **Follow-up you'll get:** "If you expect rates to fall, would you rather own a 2-year or a 30-year bond?" **The other side:** If you hold a high-quality bond to maturity, price swings along the way don't change what you get back, but as SVB showed, that only works if you're never forced to sell early.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Explain to someone who has never owned a bond why its price falls when interest rates rise.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. A $1,000 bond with a 5% coupon pays how much interest a year?
   b. Rates rise by one percentage point. Which loses more value: a 2-year bond or a 30-year bond?
   c. A bond with a $40 annual coupon trades at $950. What's its current yield?
3. **Rewind:** What is market capitalization, and how do you calculate it?
4. **Brain teaser:** A one-year zero-coupon bond pays $1,000 at maturity and yields 5%. What's its price today?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer uses a simple example: your bond pays $40 a year, new bonds pay $50, so a buyer will only pay less than $1,000 for yours, until the fixed $40 plus the gain back to $1,000 matches what new bonds offer. It avoids jargon until the idea lands.
- **a.** $50.
- **b.** The 30-year bond. It has a much longer duration.
- **c.** About 4.2%: $40 ÷ $950.
- **Rewind:** The market value of a company's equity: share price × shares outstanding.
- **Brain teaser:** About $952.38: $1,000 ÷ 1.05.
