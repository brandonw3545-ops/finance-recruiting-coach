# "Walk me through a DCF": the 60-second answer and the follow-ups
*Comes up in:* One of the most common technical questions in banking and private equity, and frequent in equity research and asset management.

## Cold open
"Walk me through a DCF."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
This question tests structure as much as knowledge. Interviewers want the steps in order, in about a minute, with the right cash flow matched to the right discount rate. Then they'll probe with follow-ups. Think of it as giving directions: the order of the turns matters more than describing every building.

## How it works
The structure, in six steps:

1. **Project** unlevered free cash flow for five to ten years.
2. **Estimate a terminal value**, with perpetuity growth or an exit multiple.
3. **Discount** both to today at WACC.
4. **Add them up** to get enterprise value.
5. **Bridge to equity value** by subtracting net debt and other claims, then divide by diluted shares.
6. **Sensitize** the result to WACC, growth and the exit multiple.

And the follow-ups you're likely to get:

| Follow-up | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Rates rise: what happens? | WACC rises and value falls, especially the terminal value |
| Levered or unlevered? | Unlevered with WACC gives EV; levered with the cost of equity gives equity value |
| What's the mid-year convention? | Discounting each year's cash flow as if it arrives mid-year, which raises value slightly |
| Which assumptions matter most? | The terminal value inputs and WACC, then growth and margins |
| When is a DCF a poor tool? | Early startups with no predictable cash flow, and banks |
| How do you get to a share price? | Subtract net debt and other claims, then divide by diluted shares |

## In the real world
You can read real DCFs for free. When a public company agrees to be sold, its proxy statement, filed with the SEC, summarizes its bankers' fairness opinions, and those summaries usually include a discounted cash flow analysis with the discount rates, terminal growth rates or exit multiples and the resulting value ranges. Search SEC EDGAR for a recent merger proxy (the filing type is DEFM14A) and read the section on the financial advisors' opinions. You'll see exactly how professionals frame each step, and the ranges they're comfortable defending.

## The interview trap
The traps are rambling past 90 seconds, skipping the terminal value or the net debt step, and reciting formulas without a structure.

> A DCF values a company based on the present value of its future cash flows. First, I'd project unlevered free cash flow for five to ten years: revenue growth and margins to get operating income, then subtract taxes, add back depreciation, and subtract capex and the investment in working capital. Second, I'd calculate a terminal value for the years after that, using either the perpetuity growth method or an exit multiple of EBITDA. Third, I'd discount the cash flows and the terminal value back to today using the weighted average cost of capital, with the cost of equity from CAPM and the after-tax cost of debt. The sum is the enterprise value. Then I'd subtract net debt to get equity value, divide by diluted shares for an implied share price, and run sensitivity tables on the discount rate and terminal assumptions.

## Your line
"Project the cash flows, add a terminal value, discount at WACC, bridge to equity, then test the assumptions." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Which assumption would you stress-test first?" **The other side:** Many bankers trust comps and precedent transactions more than a DCF for pricing a deal, and use the DCF as a cross-check.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Walk me through a DCF in under 60 seconds.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What's the mid-year convention?
   b. How do you get from enterprise value to a share price?
   c. Name a type of company for which a DCF works poorly.
3. **Rewind:** What are the two ways to calculate terminal value?
4. **Brain teaser:** A company's EV is $900, its net debt is $300 and it has 60 shares. What's the implied share price?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer hits the six steps in order, pairs unlevered free cash flow with WACC, includes the terminal value and the bridge to equity value, mentions sensitivity tables, and finishes in under 60 seconds.
- **a.** Discounting each year's cash flow as if it arrives in the middle of the year rather than the end, since cash comes in throughout the year.
- **b.** Subtract net debt and other claims, like preferred stock and noncontrolling interests, from enterprise value to get equity value, then divide by diluted shares.
- **c.** An early-stage startup with no predictable cash flow, or a bank, whose debt is part of its operations.
- **Rewind:** The perpetuity growth method and the exit multiple method.
- **Brain teaser:** $10: ($900 − $300) ÷ 60.
