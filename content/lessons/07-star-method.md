# Telling stories that prove it: the STAR method and your story bank
*Comes up in:* Every behavioral question ("Tell me about a time...") in every seat, usually one to three per interview.

## Cold open
"Tell me about a time you led a team through a challenge."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
A behavioral question is a request for evidence: prove you have this trait. **STAR** keeps the proof tight: Situation, Task, Action, Result. A **story bank** of six to eight prepared stories lets you answer almost any behavioral question by picking the right story instead of inventing one on the spot. Think of it as a deck of cards you deal from, rather than a hand you have to make up every time.

## How it works
Give each part of STAR a time budget, about 60 to 90 seconds in total.

| Part | What it covers | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | The context, in one or two sentences | About 10 seconds |
| Task | Your responsibility or the goal | About 10 seconds |
| Action | What you did, with "I" not "we," in two or three specific steps | About 40 seconds |
| Result | The outcome with a number, plus what you learned | About 15 seconds |

To build the bank, list your stories down the side of a page and common themes across the top: leadership, teamwork, conflict, failure, pressure, initiative, persuasion and attention to detail. Check the boxes each story covers. A good story covers two or three themes, and every theme should have at least one story.

Here's a fictional example. Situation: our investment club's pitch competition was two weeks away and our model had errors. Task: as team lead, I had to get us to a pitch we could defend. Action: I split the model into sections with an owner for each, set two check-ins, and rebuilt the revenue forecast myself with one teammate. Result: we placed second of twelve teams, and I learned to set check-ins early instead of assuming work was on track.

## In the real world
Big employers say outright that they want this structure. Amazon's interview guidance tells candidates to answer behavioral questions using the STAR method, and banks and consulting firms use the same kind of structured "tell me about a time" questions in first rounds and superdays. The format exists because interviewers compare candidates on the same traits, and specific stories are far easier to compare than general claims. Start your bank today: whenever something hard happens in a job, club or team, write three lines about it while you still remember the details.

## The interview trap
The traps are spending most of the time on the situation, saying "we" so the interviewer can't tell what you did, and stopping without a result.

> Our investment club's pitch competition was two weeks away, and our team's model had errors that would have sunk us in the Q&A. As team lead, my job was to get us to a pitch we could defend. I split the model into four sections with one owner each, set check-ins for day three and day eight so problems surfaced early, and rebuilt the revenue forecast myself with one teammate, since that's where most of the errors were. We fixed every issue a day before the deadline and placed second out of twelve teams. The lesson I took was to set check-ins at the start instead of assuming work is on track, and I've done that on every project since.

## Your line
"A good behavioral answer spends most of its time on what I did, and ends with a number and a lesson." **Follow-up you'll get:** "What would you do differently next time?" **The other side:** Over-rehearsed STAR stories can sound robotic. Know the beats, not a script.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Tell your best leadership story in STAR format in under 90 seconds.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Which part of STAR should take the most time?
   b. Why say "I" instead of "we"?
   c. How many stories should a story bank have?
3. **Rewind:** What should the last sentence of your "tell me about yourself" do?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's the probability of rolling at least one six in two rolls of a fair die?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer sets up the situation briefly, makes your personal role clear, describes specific actions in the first person, gives a measurable result and one honest lesson, and finishes in under 90 seconds.
- **a.** The Action.
- **b.** The interviewer is evaluating you, and "we" hides what you personally contributed.
- **c.** About six to eight, each covering two or three themes.
- **Rewind:** Say why this seat and firm are your logical next step, which hands the interview back to them.
- **Brain teaser:** 11/36, or about 31%. The chance of no six in two rolls is (5/6)² = 25/36, so the chance of at least one is 1 − 25/36.
