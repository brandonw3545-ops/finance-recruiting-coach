# Behavioral finance: loss aversion, recency bias, and the advisor as behavior coach
*Comes up in:* Wealth management interviews, client scenario questions, and "what does a good advisor actually do?"

## Cold open
"If index funds are cheap and easy, what is a financial advisor for?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Classic finance assumes investors are calm and rational. **Behavioral finance** studies what they really do, which is make the same predictable mistakes under pressure. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky laid the groundwork in a 1979 paper, and Kahneman won a Nobel Prize in 2002 for it. For wealth managers the lesson is practical: a good plan only works if the client stays in it, so a large part of the job is coaching behavior.

## How it works
The biases that come up most in interviews:

| Bias | What it is | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Loss aversion | A loss hurts about twice as much as the same gain feels good | Selling after a crash to stop the pain |
| Recency bias | Assuming the recent past will continue | Piling into whatever just went up |
| Overconfidence | Overrating your own skill | Trading too much, holding too few stocks |
| Anchoring | Fixating on a reference number | "I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid" |
| Herding | Doing what everyone else is doing | Buying at the top of a bubble |
| Home bias | Preferring the familiar | Owning mostly your own country or employer |

Loss aversion has a simple test. Offer someone a coin flip: heads they win $150, tails they lose $100. The bet is worth $25 on average, and most people still turn it down.

Put a few of these together and you get the classic pattern: investors buy after prices rise and sell after they fall, so they earn less than the funds they own. Studies that compare fund returns with what fund investors actually earned keep finding that gap.

Advisors have a few tools. They write the plan down while markets are calm. They rebalance by rule. They talk in terms of the client's goals, not this month's return. And they pick up the phone during a crash.

## In the real world
In the first weeks of the pandemic, in February and March 2020, the S&P 500 fell about 34% in just over a month. Fear was everywhere and many investors moved to cash. The index was back at a record high by August. An investor who sold in March and waited for things to feel safe missed most of that recovery. The advisors who earned their fee that year were the ones who kept clients from acting. Try it on yourself: think of the last time you wanted to sell something, or buy something, because of how it had just moved. Which bias was that?

## The interview trap
The trap is saying an advisor's job is picking investments that beat the market.

> Picking funds is the easy part and it's nearly free now. An advisor earns their fee on everything around it: building a plan that fits the client's goals and taxes, and then keeping the client in it. People are loss averse, so they want to sell after a crash, and they chase whatever just went up. That behavior costs more than fees do. A good advisor sets the plan in calm markets, rebalances by rule, and is the person who talks a client out of selling at the bottom. One conversation like that can be worth years of fees.

## Your line
"The biggest risk in most portfolios is the person who owns it." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Tell me about a bias you've caught in your own investing." **The other side:** Skeptics say "behavior coaching" is hard to measure and can be a story advisors tell to justify a 1% fee.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** A client calls during a 20% market drop and says "sell everything." Walk through the call.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What is loss aversion?
   b. Which bias is "it's gone up all year, so it'll keep going"?
   c. Who won the 2002 Nobel Prize for this work?
3. **Rewind:** What's the difference between a client's willingness to take risk and their capacity for it?
4. **Brain teaser:** A coin flip pays $150 on heads and costs $100 on tails. What's it worth on average?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Listen first and name the fear. Then go back to their plan: what the money is for, when they need it, and that the plan already assumed drops like this. Show what selling would lock in, and offer a smaller step if they need to act. A strong answer stays calm and never lectures.
- **a.** Losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains feel good, so people take poor decisions to avoid them.
- **b.** Recency bias.
- **c.** Daniel Kahneman.
- **Rewind:** Willingness is how much loss they can stomach emotionally. Capacity is how much their finances and timeline can absorb.
- **Brain teaser:** $25.
