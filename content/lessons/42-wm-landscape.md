# The wealth management landscape: wirehouses, RIAs, private banks, and how each gets paid
*Comes up in:* Wealth management and private banking interviews, especially "why this firm?" and "how do we make money?"

## Cold open
"What's the difference between a wirehouse and an RIA?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Wealth management firms all do roughly the same job, which is advising people on their money. They differ in who owns the firm, which clients they chase, and how they get paid. Knowing the map is how you answer "why us?" without sounding like you'd say the same thing anywhere.

## How it works
The main types:

| Type | Examples | Typical client | How it's paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wirehouse | Morgan Stanley, Merrill, UBS, Wells Fargo Advisors | Affluent to very wealthy | Fees on assets, plus commissions and lending |
| Private bank | J.P. Morgan Private Bank, Goldman Sachs Private Wealth | Very wealthy, often $10 million and up | Fees on assets, lending, deposits |
| Independent RIA | Thousands of small and mid-size firms | Varies widely | Mostly a fee on assets |
| Discount and digital | Schwab, Fidelity, Vanguard, robo-advisors | Everyone | Low fees, cash balances, fund fees |

A **wirehouse** is a large national brokerage whose advisors are employees. The firm supplies the brand, research, products and technology, and keeps a share of what each advisor brings in.

An **RIA**, or registered investment adviser, is a firm registered to give advice for a fee. Most are independent and owned by their advisors, and they keep client assets at a custodian such as Schwab or Fidelity.

A **private bank** sits inside a big bank and serves its wealthiest clients with investing, lending, trusts and estate planning in one place.

The most common pricing is a fee on **assets under management**. At 1% a year, a $2 million client pays $20,000 a year. Notice what that does to incentives: the advisor earns more when the account grows, which lines them up with the client, and also when the client keeps money invested with them instead of paying off a mortgage, which doesn't.

The other models are commissions on each trade or product, flat or hourly fees, and interest on loans made against the portfolio.

## In the real world
Morgan Stanley is the clearest case of a bank betting on this business. It took control of Smith Barney from Citigroup in a joint venture in 2009, finished buying it in 2013, and bought E*Trade in 2020 for about $13 billion. The logic was that fees on client assets arrive every quarter, whatever the deal market is doing, while trading and banking revenue swings. For your own recruiting, look up one firm you like and work out which box it sits in and who its typical client is.

## The interview trap
The trap is describing every firm the same way, or not knowing how the firm you're interviewing with earns its money.

> A wirehouse is a large national brokerage, like Morgan Stanley or Merrill, where advisors are employees and the firm provides the platform, research and products. An RIA is a registered investment adviser, usually independent and advisor-owned, that charges a fee on assets and holds client money at a custodian like Schwab. The big differences are scale and independence: a wirehouse offers lending, banking and access to deals, while an RIA sells itself on having fewer conflicts. Both are mostly paid a percentage of client assets.

## Your line
"Most of this industry is paid a percentage of assets, so the first question about any firm is whose assets and how they win them." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Why are advisors leaving wirehouses to go independent?" **The other side:** Critics say a fee on assets charges a client more each year for roughly the same work as their account grows.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Why would a big bank want a large wealth management business?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Name two wirehouses.
   b. What does AUM stand for?
   c. A client has $3 million at a 1% fee. What do they pay a year?
3. **Rewind:** What's the difference between asset management and wealth management?
4. **Brain teaser:** An advisor keeps 40% of the fees from $150 million of client assets charged at 1%. What do they earn a year?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** The revenue is steady. Fees on client assets come in every quarter and don't depend on deals or trading, so investors value them highly. Wealthy clients also bring deposits and borrow, and they're slow to leave. A strong answer names a real example, like Morgan Stanley.
- **a.** Any two of Morgan Stanley, Merrill, UBS and Wells Fargo Advisors.
- **b.** Assets under management.
- **c.** $30,000.
- **Rewind:** Asset managers run funds and portfolios and are judged on performance. Wealth managers advise individual clients on their whole financial picture and choose among those funds.
- **Brain teaser:** $600,000.
