# Diversification and correlation: the only free lunch in investing
*Comes up in:* Asset management and wealth management interviews, and portfolio questions in sales and trading.

## Cold open
"Why is diversification called the only free lunch in investing?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Combining investments that don't move in lockstep lowers a portfolio's ups and downs without lowering its expected return. The key measure is **correlation**, a number from −1 to +1 that describes how closely two investments move together. Harry Markowitz worked this out in a 1952 paper, the start of modern portfolio theory, and later shared a Nobel Prize for it; the "free lunch" line is usually credited to him. Think of a shop that sells both umbrellas and sunscreen: its sales are steadier than a shop that sells only one, whatever the weather.

## How it works
Take two investments, each expected to return 8% a year with the same volatility, a standard deviation of 20%, and split your money 50/50 between them:

| Correlation | Portfolio's expected return | Portfolio's volatility |
|---|---|---|
| +1 (move in lockstep) | 8% | 20.0% |
| +0.5 | 8% | 17.3% |
| 0 (unrelated) | 8% | 14.1% |
| −1 (exact opposites) | 8% | 0% |

The expected return is 8% in every case; only the risk changes. That's the free lunch.

There are two kinds of risk:

- **Company-specific risk:** a failed product, a fraud, a lawsuit. Diversification can mostly remove it. Owning a few dozen stocks across different industries eliminates much of it.
- **Market risk:** recessions, interest rate shocks, panics. It hits nearly everything at once, so diversification can't remove it. That's why investors expect to be paid for bearing it, and why beta, from the WACC lesson, measures only this kind of risk.

The catch: correlations tend to rise during a crisis, just when you need them low. In the fall of 2008, almost everything except Treasuries fell together.

## In the real world
At the end of 2000, 62% of the money in Enron's 401(k) retirement plan was invested in Enron's own stock. A year later, the company had collapsed in an accounting fraud and filed for bankruptcy, and the stock was nearly worthless. Many employees lost their jobs and their retirement savings at the same time, because both depended on the same company: about as correlated as two risks can be. The lesson stuck: don't concentrate your savings in the company that also pays your salary. If you help run a student investment fund or a paper portfolio, check how much of it would fall at once if a single sector crashed.

## The interview trap
The traps are saying diversification means owning lots of stocks (30 tech stocks are still one big bet) and claiming it eliminates all risk.

> Diversification lowers a portfolio's risk without necessarily lowering its expected return, as long as the assets don't move perfectly together. That's measured by correlation, which runs from −1 to +1. If two assets with the same expected return and volatility have a correlation below 1, a mix of them has the same expected return but lower volatility, because their ups and downs partly cancel out. It removes company-specific risk, like one firm's fraud or failed product, but not market risk, like a recession that hits everything, which is why investors are paid for bearing market risk. And it's not about the number of holdings: 30 tech stocks are still one big bet, and correlations tend to rise in a crisis.

## Your line
"What matters isn't how many things you own, but how differently they move." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Why do correlations rise in a crisis, and what does that mean for diversification?" **The other side:** Concentrated investors like Warren Buffett argue that diversification mainly protects against not knowing what you own, and that spreading money across many holdings dilutes your best ideas.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Is a portfolio of 30 technology stocks diversified? Explain.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What does a correlation of +1 mean?
   b. Which kind of risk can't be diversified away?
   c. Two assets each have 20% volatility and zero correlation. What's the volatility of a 50/50 mix?
3. **Rewind:** What job does each of the main asset classes do in a portfolio?
4. **Brain teaser:** Roughly what is the square root of 0.02?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Not really. It removes some company-specific risk, but the stocks share the same industry risks, like interest rates, regulation and tech spending cycles, so they tend to move together. A strong answer explains that diversification depends on correlation, not the number of holdings.
- **a.** The two always move in the same direction, in proportion.
- **b.** Market risk, also called systematic risk.
- **c.** About 14%.
- **Rewind:** Stocks for long-term growth, bonds for income and stability, cash for safety and liquidity, real estate for income and inflation protection, and alternatives for returns that don't move with public markets.
- **Brain teaser:** About 0.141.
