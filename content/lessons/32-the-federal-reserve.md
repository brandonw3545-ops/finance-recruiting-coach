# The Federal Reserve: the fed funds rate, the FOMC and the dual mandate
*Comes up in:* Almost every markets conversation, from sales and trading to wealth management, and in "what's happening in the markets" questions.

## Cold open
"What does the Fed do, and how does a rate hike actually slow the economy?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
The Federal Reserve is the U.S. central bank. Congress gave it a **dual mandate**: maximum employment and stable prices, which the Fed defines as 2% inflation. Its main tool is the **federal funds rate**, the interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. The Fed doesn't set your mortgage or car loan rate directly, but moving the shortest rate in the economy ripples out to all the others. Think of a thermostat in a big building: turn it up to cool an overheating economy, down to warm a cold one, knowing the rooms take months to respond.

## How it works
The **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)** sets policy. It has 12 voting members: the seven governors of the Federal Reserve Board, the president of the New York Fed, and four of the other eleven regional Fed presidents, who rotate each year. It meets eight times a year and sets a target range for the fed funds rate, like 4.25% to 4.50%. It keeps the market rate inside that range mainly through the interest it pays banks on the reserves they hold at the Fed.

Its other tools: buying bonds to push long-term rates down (**quantitative easing**, or QE), shrinking its bond holdings to let them rise (**quantitative tightening**, or QT), and **forward guidance**, signaling where rates are headed.

How a rate hike works its way through the economy:

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 | The Fed raises its target range |
| 2 | Short-term rates rise: credit cards, adjustable-rate loans, savings accounts, Treasury bills |
| 3 | Long-term rates, like mortgages, often rise too, if markets expect rates to stay high |
| 4 | Borrowing costs more, so households and companies spend and invest less |
| 5 | Stock and bond prices tend to fall and the dollar strengthens, cooling demand further |
| 6 | Hiring slows and, with a lag of months, so does inflation |

Rates are quoted in **basis points**: one basis point is 0.01%, so a "quarter-point" hike is 25 basis points.

## In the real world
From March 2022 to July 2023, the FOMC raised its target range from 0%–0.25% to 5.25%–5.50%, the fastest run of increases since the early 1980s, including four straight hikes of three-quarters of a point in 2022. It started cutting in September 2024. Markets now hang on every meeting: the statement, the "dot plot" of officials' rate projections, released four times a year, and the chair's press conference. Before any interview, know three things: the current target range, who chairs the Fed, and what markets expect at the next meeting. The CME FedWatch tool shows market-implied odds for free.

## The interview trap
The traps are saying the Fed sets mortgage rates, describing its job as fighting inflation alone, and not being able to explain how a rate hike actually reaches the economy.

> The Federal Reserve is the U.S. central bank, and Congress gave it a dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices, which it defines as 2% inflation. Its main tool is the federal funds rate, the overnight rate banks charge each other, which the FOMC sets as a target range at its eight meetings a year. When the Fed raises that rate, borrowing costs rise across the economy, from credit cards to business loans and often mortgages, so households and companies spend and invest less, asset prices tend to fall and the dollar strengthens. That cools demand, which over time slows hiring and inflation. It can also buy or shrink its holdings of bonds, known as quantitative easing and tightening, and it uses forward guidance to shape what markets expect.

## Your line
"The Fed controls the shortest interest rate in the economy; markets set the rest." **Follow-up you'll get:** "What's the risk of raising rates too far, or cutting too early?" **The other side:** The Fed's tools work with long and variable lags, so it's always steering partly by the rear-view mirror; critics say it moved too late in 2021.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** How does a Fed rate hike slow inflation? Walk through it step by step.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What are the two parts of the dual mandate?
   b. How many times a year does the FOMC meet?
   c. What is quantitative tightening?
3. **Rewind:** What's the difference between CPI and PCE, and which does the Fed target?
4. **Brain teaser:** The Fed's target range went from 0%–0.25% to 5.25%–5.50%. How many basis points is that?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer goes in order: the Fed raises the fed funds rate; short-term borrowing costs rise, and often long-term ones too; households and businesses borrow, spend and invest less; asset prices fall and the dollar strengthens; demand cools, hiring slows, and inflation eases after a lag of months.
- **a.** Maximum employment and stable prices.
- **b.** Eight.
- **c.** The Fed shrinking its balance sheet, mainly by letting bonds mature without replacing them, which tends to push long-term interest rates up.
- **Rewind:** CPI, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, tracks what urban consumers pay out of pocket; PCE, from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, is broader and adjusts as people switch products. The Fed targets 2% PCE inflation.
- **Brain teaser:** 525 basis points.
