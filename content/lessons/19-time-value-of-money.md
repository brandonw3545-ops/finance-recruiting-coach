# Time value of money: why $100 today beats $100 next year
*Comes up in:* The foundation of every valuation question, and a common warm-up in wealth management and asset management interviews.

## Cold open
"Would you rather have $100 today or $105 a year from now? What does your answer depend on?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
A dollar today is worth more than a dollar later, for three reasons. You can invest today's dollar and earn a return. Inflation erodes what a future dollar can buy. And a promised future dollar might not arrive. So before you compare money at different dates, you move it to the same date: forward by compounding, or back to today by discounting. Think of today's dollar as a seed you can plant now, and next year's dollar as a seedling someone promised to hand you later.

## How it works
Two formulas do all the work:

- **Future value** = present value × (1 + r)ⁿ
- **Present value** = future value ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ

At 5%, $100 today grows to $105 in one year and $110.25 in two. Going the other way, $105 received in one year is worth exactly $100 today at a 5% rate, but only about $97.22 at 8%. The higher the rate you could earn elsewhere, the less a future payment is worth today.

Compounding is what makes time so powerful. Here's $1,000 growing at 7% a year:

| Years | Value |
|---|---|
| 10 | $1,967 |
| 20 | $3,870 |
| 30 | $7,612 |

A handy shortcut is the **rule of 72**: divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate how many years money takes to double. At 7%, that's a little over 10 years, which matches the table.

## In the real world
The rate you could earn elsewhere changed dramatically between 2021 and 2023. When the Fed raised rates, yields on three-month Treasury bills went from near zero to above 5%. Suddenly $100 today could earn 5% with almost no risk, so a promise of $105 next year stopped looking generous. The same math hit asset prices: in 2022, as rates rose, the Nasdaq fell about 33% and the broad U.S. bond market lost about 13%, its worst year on record, because future cash flows were worth less in today's dollars. Before an interview, look up the current three-month Treasury bill yield; it's the simplest answer to "what could I earn safely?"

## The interview trap
The trap is answering "the $105, because it's more" without comparing it to anything, or forgetting that the future payment carries risk.

> It depends on what I could do with the $100 today. Money today is worth more than the same amount later because I could invest it, because inflation erodes what future dollars can buy, and because a future payment carries some risk of not arriving. So I'd compare the 5% return implied by the $105 with what I could earn on something equally safe. If one-year Treasury bills pay more than 5%, I'd take the $100 now and invest it; if they pay less, the $105 is the better deal, as long as I'm confident it will be paid. In general, the present value of a future amount is that amount divided by one plus the discount rate, raised to the number of years.

## Your line
"You can only compare dollars once you've moved them to the same date." **Follow-up you'll get:** "What discount rate would you use, and why?" **The other side:** People often prefer money now even when waiting pays more, which is why real decisions don't always follow the formula.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Explain to a friend with no finance background why $1,000 in five years is worth less than $1,000 today.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What is $100 growing at 10% a year worth in two years?
   b. What's the present value of $110 received in one year at a 10% discount rate?
   c. Using the rule of 72, about how long does money take to double at 9%?
3. **Rewind:** What's the difference between levered and unlevered free cash flow?
4. **Brain teaser:** What is 1.05 squared?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer gives all three reasons in plain words (you could invest it, inflation shrinks what it buys, and the future payment might not come) and uses a simple example, like $1,000 invested at 5% growing to about $1,276 in five years.
- **a.** $121.
- **b.** $100.
- **c.** About 8 years: 72 ÷ 9.
- **Rewind:** Unlevered free cash flow is before interest and belongs to all capital providers; levered free cash flow is after interest and debt repayment and belongs to shareholders.
- **Brain teaser:** 1.1025.
