# How the three statements link together
*Comes up in:* Banking and private equity technicals, and every financial modeling test.

## Cold open
"How do the three financial statements link together?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Net income from the income statement is the thread. It starts the cash flow statement and adds to retained earnings on the balance sheet. The cash flow statement then explains why each balance sheet account changed, ending with the change in cash. Think of the income statement as the movie of the year, the balance sheet as a photo taken at the end, and the cash flow statement as the explanation of how cash got from the first photo to the second.

## How it works
Six links do almost all the work:

1. **Net income** starts the cash flow statement and, minus dividends, adds to retained earnings.
2. **Depreciation** is added back on the cash flow statement and reduces PP&E on the balance sheet.
3. **Capital expenditures** appear under investing and increase PP&E.
4. **Working capital changes** on the cash flow statement match the changes in receivables, inventory and payables on the balance sheet.
5. **Financing flows** (borrowing, repaying debt, issuing or buying back stock, dividends) match the changes in debt and equity.
6. **The net change in cash**, added to beginning cash, gives the ending cash on the balance sheet.

Those links work as "roll-forwards," where each balance carries from one year to the next. Here they are for Coffee Co.:

| Account | Beginning | Plus | Minus | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | 100 | Net change in cash +40 | | 140 |
| Retained earnings | 500 | Net income +150 | Dividends 10 | 640 |
| PP&E | 800 | Capex +100 | Depreciation 50 | 850 |

## In the real world
Every banking model is built on these links, and every good one has a "balance check" row that flags an error the moment assets stop equaling liabilities plus equity. Checks exist because spreadsheet mistakes are expensive. JPMorgan's 2013 internal report on its "London Whale" trading losses, which ran to about $6 billion, found that a key risk model ran on spreadsheets with manual copy-and-paste steps and a formula error that understated risk. If you build a model in a club or a training course, add the balance check first and never hard-code a number to force it to balance.

## The interview trap
The trap is saying "they're all connected" without naming the links, or forgetting the cash link that makes the balance sheet balance.

> Net income at the bottom of the income statement flows to the top of the cash flow statement, and into retained earnings on the balance sheet after subtracting any dividends. On the cash flow statement, you adjust net income for non-cash items like depreciation and for changes in working capital, and those adjustments line up with changes in balance sheet accounts like PP&E, receivables, inventory and payables. Investing activities like capex change PP&E, and financing activities like borrowing or buying back stock change debt and equity. Finally, the net change in cash at the bottom of the cash flow statement, added to the beginning cash balance, gives the ending cash on the balance sheet, which is what makes it balance.

## Your line
"If you know the six links, you can walk any change through all three statements." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Depreciation goes up by $10. Walk me through it." **The other side:** In real filings, a few items, such as foreign currency translation, change equity without passing through net income, through what's called other comprehensive income.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** A company issues $100 of new stock for cash. Walk me through the three statements.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Beginning PP&E is 800, capex is 100 and depreciation is 50. What's ending PP&E?
   b. Where do dividends show up on each of the three statements?
   c. In a model, which link makes the balance sheet balance?
3. **Rewind:** Why does a rise in accounts receivable reduce operating cash flow?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** No effect on the income statement. On the cash flow statement, cash from financing rises by $100, so cash rises by $100. On the balance sheet, cash is up $100 and common stock (or additional paid-in capital) is up $100, so it balances.
- **a.** 850.
- **b.** Not on the income statement. On the cash flow statement, as a financing outflow. On the balance sheet, cash goes down and retained earnings goes down.
- **c.** The net change in cash from the cash flow statement, added to beginning cash, becomes ending cash on the balance sheet.
- **Rewind:** The revenue was recorded, but the customer hasn't paid yet, so the cash hasn't come in.
- **Brain teaser:** 1.331. 1.1 × 1.1 = 1.21, and 1.21 × 1.1 = 1.331.
