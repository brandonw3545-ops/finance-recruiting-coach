# Equity value vs. enterprise value (the house-and-mortgage analogy)
*Comes up in:* Nearly every banking, private equity and equity research first round, usually as one of the first technical questions.

## Cold open
"What's the difference between equity value and enterprise value?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
**Equity value** is what the shares are worth: the share price times diluted shares outstanding. It belongs to shareholders. **Enterprise value (EV)** is what the core business is worth to everyone who funds it: equity value plus debt and other investor claims, minus cash. Think of a house worth $500,000 with a $400,000 mortgage. The owner's equity is $100,000, but the house itself is worth $500,000. If you bought it, you'd pay the owner $100,000 and take on the $400,000 mortgage, so the total cost is $500,000.

## How it works
Here's a company with a $50 share price.

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Share price | $50 |
| × Diluted shares | 10 million |
| **= Equity value** | **$500 million** |
| + Debt | $200 million |
| − Cash | $50 million |
| **= Enterprise value** | **$650 million** |

Why bother with EV? Because buying an entire business means taking on its debt, and because it lets you compare companies with different amounts of debt on an equal footing. It also tells you which metrics to pair it with. EV represents all investors, so it goes with measures calculated before interest, like revenue and EBITDA. Equity value represents only shareholders, so it goes with measures after interest, like net income and earnings per share.

## In the real world
When Broadcom agreed to buy VMware in May 2022, its announcement gave both numbers. It would pay about $61 billion in cash and stock for VMware's shares, its equity value, and assume $8 billion of VMware's net debt, putting the total enterprise value at roughly $69 billion. The deal closed in November 2023. Headlines often quote one number without saying which it is, so when you read about a deal, check whether the figure includes the target's debt.

## The interview trap
The traps are saying enterprise value is "what the company is worth to everyone" with nothing more specific, forgetting to subtract cash, or treating EV and market capitalization as the same thing.

> Equity value is the value of a company's shares: the share price times diluted shares outstanding, which is the part that belongs to shareholders. Enterprise value is the value of the company's core operations to all of its capital providers: equity value plus debt, preferred stock and noncontrolling interests, minus cash. Think of a house worth $500,000 with a $400,000 mortgage: the owner's equity is $100,000, but the house itself is worth $500,000. We use enterprise value to compare companies with different amounts of debt, and we pair it with metrics that belong to all investors, like revenue or EBITDA, while equity value pairs with metrics that belong only to shareholders, like net income.

## Your line
"Equity value is what the shareholders own; enterprise value is what the business is worth to everyone who funded it." **Follow-up you'll get:** "Can enterprise value be lower than equity value?" **The other side:** EV treats all cash as surplus, but some cash is needed to run the business, so some analysts subtract only the excess.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Use the house analogy to explain why you add debt to get to enterprise value.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Share price $20, 50 million diluted shares, $300 million of debt and $100 million of cash. What's EV?
   b. Can enterprise value be negative?
   c. Which goes with EBITDA: enterprise value or equity value?
3. **Rewind:** What does IRR measure?
4. **Brain teaser:** A company's share price rises 10%, and its debt and cash don't change. Does its EV rise by more or less than 10%?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A house worth $500,000 with a $400,000 mortgage has $100,000 of equity. A buyer pays the owner $100,000 and takes on the mortgage, so the house costs $500,000 in total. In the same way, buying a company means paying its shareholders and taking on its debt, so EV adds debt to equity value.
- **a.** $1.2 billion: equity value of $1 billion, plus $300 million of debt, minus $100 million of cash.
- **b.** Yes, if a company's cash is larger than its equity value plus debt, which happens occasionally with cash-rich companies the market has given up on.
- **c.** Enterprise value.
- **Rewind:** A project's implied annual return: the discount rate at which its NPV equals zero.
- **Brain teaser:** It depends on net debt. EV rises by the same dollar amount as equity value; with net debt, EV is larger, so the percentage rise is less than 10%. With net cash, it's more than 10%.
