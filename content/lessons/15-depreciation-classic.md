# The classic: "Depreciation goes up by $10. Walk me through the three statements."
*Comes up in:* Possibly the single most-asked accounting question in banking and private equity first rounds.

## Cold open
"Depreciation goes up by $10. Walk me through the three statements. Assume a 25% tax rate."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Depreciation is an expense that uses no cash but does lower taxes. So when it rises, net income falls by the after-tax amount, cash actually rises by the tax saved, PP&E falls by the full amount, and equity falls by the drop in net income. Both sides of the balance sheet fall by the same amount. Think of it as a $10 deduction that costs you nothing in cash today but cuts your tax bill by $2.50.

## How it works
Go statement by statement, always in the same order: income statement, cash flow statement, balance sheet.

| Statement | Line | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Income statement | Depreciation | +10 |
| | Pre-tax income | −10 |
| | Taxes (25%) | −2.50 |
| | **Net income** | **−7.50** |
| Cash flow statement | Net income | −7.50 |
| | Add back depreciation | +10 |
| | **Cash flow from operations and net change in cash** | **+2.50** |
| Balance sheet: assets | Cash | +2.50 |
| | PP&E | −10 |
| | **Total assets** | **−7.50** |
| Balance sheet: liabilities and equity | Retained earnings | −7.50 |
| | **Total liabilities and equity** | **−7.50** |

Check it with a different tax rate. At 40%, net income falls by $6, cash rises by $4, assets fall by $6 and equity falls by $6. The pattern never changes: net income falls by the change times (1 − tax rate), and cash rises by the change times the tax rate. Amortization works exactly the same way.

## In the real world
The tax shield from depreciation is real money, and Congress uses it as a policy lever. The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act let companies deduct the full cost of many equipment purchases in the first year, called 100% bonus depreciation, which pulled tax savings forward and encouraged investment. The rules have changed since, so check current law before you quote them. In practice, companies keep two sets of depreciation, one for financial reporting and one for taxes, and the gap between them creates deferred taxes, a topic for later.

## The interview trap
The traps are forgetting taxes (saying net income falls by $10), saying cash goes down, or getting lost because you didn't go statement by statement.

> On the income statement, depreciation goes up by $10, so pre-tax income falls by $10, and with a 25% tax rate, net income falls by $7.50. On the cash flow statement, net income is down $7.50, but depreciation is a non-cash expense, so you add back the $10, and cash flow from operations is up $2.50; that's the tax savings. On the balance sheet, cash is up $2.50 and PP&E is down $10 from the extra depreciation, so total assets are down $7.50. On the other side, retained earnings is down $7.50 because of the lower net income, so liabilities and equity are also down $7.50, and the balance sheet balances.

## Your line
"Depreciation doesn't cost cash; it saves taxes." **Follow-up you'll get:** "What changes if the tax rate is 40%?" **The other side:** In real life, book and tax depreciation often differ, so the cash tax savings may not match the expense on the income statement in the same year.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Walk me through a $20 increase in depreciation with a 40% tax rate.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Why does cash go up when depreciation increases?
   b. With a 25% tax rate, what happens to net income if amortization rises by $40?
   c. In the standard answer, do liabilities change?
3. **Rewind:** What's the difference between capex and depreciation?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's 12.5% of 64?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Income statement: pre-tax income falls by $20, taxes fall by $8, net income falls by $12. Cash flow statement: net income −12, add back depreciation +20, so cash is up $8. Balance sheet: cash +8 and PP&E −20, so assets are down $12; retained earnings is down $12, so it balances.
- **a.** Depreciation lowers taxable income, so the company pays less tax, and the expense itself uses no cash.
- **b.** It falls by $30.
- **c.** No. Liabilities would only change if you were modeling taxes payable or deferred taxes.
- **Rewind:** Capex is the cash spent to buy long-lived assets, shown under investing and added to PP&E; depreciation spreads that cost over the asset's life on the income statement, as a non-cash expense that gets added back.
- **Brain teaser:** 8. 12.5% is one-eighth, and 64 ÷ 8 = 8.
