# The map of finance: sell side vs. buy side, and where every seat fits
*Comes up in:* Almost every first conversation, as "What do you know about the industry?" or "Why this seat over the others?"

## Cold open
"Walk me through the difference between the sell side and the buy side."

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Finance is a marketplace for money. The **sell side** helps companies and investors get things done, like raising money, buying a company or trading a security, and it gets paid fees, commissions or trading spreads for the service. The **buy side** has money to invest and gets paid based on how much it manages and how well it invests. Think of a house sale: the sell side is the real estate agent, paid when the deal closes. The buy side is the buyer, who lives with the result.

## How it works
Here's where each seat sits, what a first-year actually does, and how the firm makes money.

| Seat | What a first-year does | How the firm gets paid |
|---|---|---|
| Investment banking (sell) | Builds models and pitch books for mergers, IPOs and debt deals | Advisory and underwriting fees, mostly when deals close |
| Sales & trading (sell) | Quotes prices, manages risk, talks markets with clients | Bid-ask spreads, commissions, financing |
| Equity research (sell) | Models companies and writes reports with ratings | Supports the bank's trading and client relationships |
| Asset management (buy) | Researches stocks or bonds for funds | A percentage of assets under management |
| Hedge funds (buy) | Researches ideas for a fund that can bet up or down | A management fee plus a share of profits |
| Private equity (buy) | Evaluates and buys whole companies, often with debt | A management fee plus carried interest |
| Wealth management | Builds portfolios and plans for individuals and families | Fees on client assets, sometimes commissions |

Two patterns matter. First, the test for which side a firm is on: does it get paid for completing transactions, or for managing money? Second, the sell side hires far more undergraduates, so many people start there and move to the buy side after two or three years. Wealth management is its own world: it invests like the buy side, but its client is a person, so the work mixes portfolio decisions with planning and relationships.

## In the real world
The 2008 crisis redrew this map. In September 2008, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley converted into bank holding companies to get access to Federal Reserve support. The Volcker Rule, part of the 2010 Dodd-Frank Act, then limited banks' trading with their own money, and much of that activity moved to hedge funds. Banks went looking for steadier revenue: Morgan Stanley took over Smith Barney's brokerage between 2009 and 2013 and bought E*Trade in 2020, turning wealth management into its largest business. To see the map moving today, read a big bank's quarterly earnings release and compare how fast advisory, trading and wealth revenue are growing.

## The interview trap
Most candidates say the sell side "sells stocks" and the buy side "buys stocks." That's a guess dressed up as an answer. Define the sides by who the client is and how the firm gets paid.

> The sell side provides services to companies and investors: raising capital, advising on mergers, making markets and publishing research. It earns fees, commissions and trading spreads, mostly when transactions happen. The buy side manages money. Asset managers, hedge funds, private equity and venture firms invest capital for clients or their own funds, and they're paid a percentage of the assets plus, for hedge funds and private equity, a share of the profits. So the simplest test is whether a firm gets paid for transactions or for performance. Wealth management sits close to the buy side, but its clients are individuals and families, so the job blends portfolio work with planning. Many people start on the sell side, where firms hire more undergraduates, and move to the buy side later.

## Your line
"The cleanest way to tell the sides apart is how they get paid: the sell side for transactions, the buy side for performance." **Follow-up you'll get:** "So which side do you want to start on, and why?" **The other side:** The line blurs. Big banks run asset and wealth management arms, and the largest asset managers trade at a scale that looks a lot like the sell side.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Pick the seat you're most drawn to and explain in 60 seconds what a first-year does there and how the firm makes money.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Is equity research on the buy side or the sell side?
   b. How does a private equity firm get paid?
   c. Name two ways a sell-side trading desk makes money.
3. **Rewind:** In one sentence each, what is a stock and what is a bond?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's 15% of 360?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer names concrete first-year work (models, pitch books, client portfolios), who the client is, how the firm earns money (transaction fees vs. assets and performance), and one honest reason the seat fits you.
- **a.** The sell side. A bank's research supports its trading and client business; buy-side firms employ their own analysts.
- **b.** A management fee on the capital it manages (often around 1.5% to 2% a year) plus carried interest, usually about 20% of profits above a minimum return.
- **c.** Earning the bid-ask spread by making markets, plus commissions and financing, such as lending to hedge funds.
- **Rewind:** A stock is a slice of ownership in a company; a bond is a loan to a company or government that pays interest.
- **Brain teaser:** 54. Ten percent is 36 and five percent is 18, so 15% is 36 + 18.
