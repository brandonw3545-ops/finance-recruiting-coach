# Investment banking: what bankers do, and bulge brackets vs. elite boutiques vs. middle market
*Comes up in:* Every banking interview ("Why banking?", "Why a boutique over a bulge bracket?") and useful context for any seat.

## Cold open
"What does an investment banker actually do?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Investment bankers advise companies, and sometimes governments and investment funds, on their biggest financial decisions. They help clients buy or sell businesses, which is mergers and acquisitions (M&A), and they help them raise money by selling stock or bonds to investors, which is capital markets. Think of a real estate agent and a mortgage broker rolled into one, except the property is an entire company.

## How it works
Banks are split into **industry groups** (healthcare, technology, financial institutions and so on) and **product groups** (M&A, equity capital markets, debt capital markets, leveraged finance, restructuring). A deal moves through roughly the same steps: pitch the client, get hired, do due diligence, build the valuation and models, market the deal to buyers or investors, and close.

As an analyst, you'd build the financial models and valuation work (comparable companies, discounted cash flow, precedent transactions), make the pitch books, and keep the deal organized. Associates check and manage that work; vice presidents and managing directors run the client relationships. Banks are paid fees, mostly when a deal closes: an advisory fee on M&A, and an underwriting fee on stock and bond offerings.

| Type | Examples | What sets them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Bulge bracket | Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi | Full service and global: advisory, capital markets, trading and lending |
| Elite boutique | Evercore, Lazard, Centerview, PJT Partners, Moelis | Advisory only, senior-led teams, many large M&A and restructuring deals, no lending |
| Middle market | Houlihan Lokey, William Blair, Baird, Harris Williams | Smaller deals, often selling privately owned companies |

The typical path: a two-year analyst program, then promotion to associate or a move to private equity, a hedge fund, corporate development or growth investing.

## In the real world
When Microsoft agreed to buy Activision Blizzard for about $69 billion in January 2022, the press release listed the advisers: Goldman Sachs advised Microsoft, and Allen & Company, a boutique known for media and technology deals, advised Activision. The deal didn't close until October 2023, after months of antitrust review in the U.S., the U.K. and Europe, which is a reminder that a signed deal isn't a closed deal and that bankers often wait many months to be paid. To follow banking today, read deal announcements, which name the advisers, and check the quarterly M&A league tables.

## The interview trap
The trap is saying bankers "invest money" or "pick stocks." They don't; they advise and raise capital. The second trap is trashing a type of bank to flatter the one interviewing you.

> Investment bankers advise companies on their biggest financial decisions and help them raise capital. On the advisory side, that's mergers and acquisitions: helping a company buy another business or sell itself, which means valuing it, running the process and negotiating. On the capital markets side, banks help companies raise money by issuing stock, as in an IPO, or bonds, and they find the investors to buy them. As an analyst, you'd build the models and valuation work behind those decisions, like comparable companies and DCFs, and put together the materials clients see. Banks are mostly paid fees when deals close, which is why the job is so deal-driven and the hours follow the deals.

## Your line
"Bulge brackets win on breadth and balance sheet; elite boutiques win on senior attention and independence." **Follow-up you'll get:** "So why do you want to start at a boutique, or at a bulge bracket?" **The other side:** Boutique analysts often get more responsibility on each deal, while bulge bracket analysts see more products and build a bigger network.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Explain the difference between a bulge bracket and an elite boutique, and say which you'd rather start at and why.
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. What's the difference between ECM and DCM?
   b. When does a bank usually get paid an M&A advisory fee?
   c. Name two common next steps after a banking analyst program.
3. **Rewind:** Is investment banking on the buy side or the sell side, and how does it get paid?
4. **Brain teaser:** What's the sum of the whole numbers from 1 to 100?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** A strong answer says bulge brackets are full-service global banks with lending, trading and every product, while elite boutiques focus on advisory with senior-led teams and no lending; then gives an honest preference tied to what you want to learn, without trashing either.
- **a.** Equity capital markets helps companies raise money by selling stock, such as IPOs; debt capital markets helps them raise money by issuing bonds.
- **b.** Mostly when the deal closes, as a success fee, sometimes with a smaller retainer along the way.
- **c.** Any two of: private equity, hedge funds, corporate development, growth equity or venture capital, or promotion to associate.
- **Rewind:** The sell side; it earns advisory and underwriting fees on deals.
- **Brain teaser:** 5,050. Pair 1 with 100, 2 with 99 and so on: 50 pairs that each sum to 101.
