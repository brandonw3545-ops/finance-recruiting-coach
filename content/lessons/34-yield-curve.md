# The yield curve, and what an inversion is supposed to signal
*Comes up in:* Sales and trading and asset management interviews, and any "what's your view on the economy" question.

## Cold open
"What's an inverted yield curve, and why do people think it predicts recessions?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
The yield curve plots Treasury yields from short maturities, a few months, to long ones, up to 30 years. Normally it slopes upward, because lenders want extra yield for tying up their money longer. It **inverts** when short-term yields rise above long-term ones. That usually happens when the Fed has pushed short-term rates up to fight inflation while investors expect growth to slow and the Fed to cut later, so long-term yields stay lower. Long-term yields are roughly the market's forecast of where short-term rates will average, plus a bit extra; an inversion is the market saying today's rates are too high to last.

## How it works
The common shapes:

- **Normal:** upward sloping; long-term yields above short-term ones.
- **Flat:** little difference across maturities.
- **Inverted:** short-term yields above long-term ones.
- **Steep:** long-term yields well above short-term ones, often early in a recovery, after the Fed has cut.

| Maturity | A normal curve (illustration) | An inverted curve (roughly mid-2023) |
|---|---|---|
| 3 months | 2.0% | 5.4% |
| 2 years | 2.5% | 4.9% |
| 10 years | 3.5% | 3.9% |
| 30 years | 3.8% | 4.0% |

The two most-watched spreads are the 10-year yield minus the 2-year, and the 10-year minus the 3-month, which the New York Fed uses in its recession model. They're quoted in basis points.

Why might an inversion predict a recession?
1. **Expectations:** markets expect the Fed to cut, which it usually does when the economy weakens.
2. **Bank lending:** banks borrow short, through deposits, and lend long. When short rates are above long ones, lending earns less, so credit tightens.
3. **Tight policy:** it shows the Fed holding short rates well above where markets think they'll settle.

Before 2022, an inversion had come before every U.S. recession since the 1950s, with one false alarm in the mid-1960s, usually six months to two years ahead.

## In the real world
The 10-year minus 2-year spread inverted in July 2022 and stayed inverted for about two years, an unusually long stretch, reaching about a full percentage point below zero at its deepest in July 2023. It turned positive again in September 2024. Yet the U.S. economy kept growing through 2023 and 2024, and unemployment stayed low. Was the signal broken, or just slow? Some argue pandemic-era savings and the Fed's huge bond purchases distorted long-term yields; others point out that past recessions often began after the curve had already turned positive again. It's a favorite discussion question because there's no settled answer. Check today's curve on the Treasury's daily yield curve page and decide whether it's normal, flat or inverted.

## The interview trap
The traps are saying an inversion causes recessions, treating it as a precise timer, and getting backwards which yield is higher.

> The yield curve plots Treasury yields across maturities. Normally it slopes up, because investors want extra yield for lending for longer. It inverts when short-term yields rise above long-term ones, which usually happens when the Fed has raised short rates to fight inflation while investors expect growth to slow and rates to come down later, so long-term yields stay lower. It's watched because an inversion has come before nearly every U.S. recession since the 1950s, usually by six months to two years, and because it squeezes banks, which borrow short and lend long, so credit can tighten. But it's a signal, not a cause or a timer: the curve inverted in 2022, and the economy kept growing through 2024.

## Your line
"An inversion is the bond market betting the Fed will have to cut, usually because growth is about to slow." **Follow-up you'll get:** "The curve inverted in 2022 and no recession followed right away. Why might that be?" **The other side:** Some argue the extra yield investors demand for long-term bonds has shrunk for structural reasons, like central bank bond buying and global demand for Treasuries, making inversions less informative than they used to be.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Why might an inverted yield curve signal a coming recession?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. The 2-year Treasury yields 4.8% and the 10-year yields 4.2%. What's the spread, and is the curve inverted?
   b. What shape is a normal yield curve?
   c. Why do banks dislike an inverted curve?
3. **Rewind:** Give two reasons stocks often fall when interest rates rise.
4. **Brain teaser:** How many basis points are in 1.25 percentage points?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** An inversion means markets expect short-term rates to fall, which usually happens when the Fed cuts because the economy is weakening. It also squeezes banks, which borrow short and lend long, so they lend less. A strong answer adds that it's a signal with a long and variable lag, not a cause, and mentions the 2022–2024 inversion that wasn't quickly followed by a recession.
- **a.** −60 basis points; yes, it's inverted.
- **b.** Upward sloping.
- **c.** They pay short-term rates on deposits and earn long-term rates on loans, so an inversion squeezes their profit margin.
- **Rewind:** Any two of: future profits are discounted at a higher rate; safe bonds become more attractive competition; and higher borrowing costs slow the economy and cut earnings.
- **Brain teaser:** 125.
