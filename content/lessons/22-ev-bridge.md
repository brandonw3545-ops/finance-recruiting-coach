# The EV bridge: why you add debt and subtract cash (and the trick questions)
*Comes up in:* Banking and private equity technicals, often as a follow-up to the enterprise value question, with a trick question attached.

## Cold open
"Why do you add debt and subtract cash to get from equity value to enterprise value?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Enterprise value adds every claim on the business and subtracts everything that isn't part of the business. If you bought a whole company, you'd pay the shareholders and then also have to take on or repay its debt and other investor claims, so those get added. You'd also get its cash, which you could use to pay down that debt, so it reduces your real cost and gets subtracted. Think of buying a used car that comes with a $2,000 loan attached and $300 in the glove box: the real price is what you pay the seller, plus the loan, minus the $300.

## How it works
Here's the bridge, item by item:

| Item | Direction | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Equity value | Start | The value to common shareholders |
| + Debt | Add | Lenders' claim; a buyer must repay or assume it |
| + Preferred stock | Add | Another investor claim, ranked ahead of common shareholders |
| + Noncontrolling interests | Add | The parent reports 100% of a subsidiary's EBITDA but owns less, so add the outside owners' stake to match |
| − Cash | Subtract | It isn't part of operations, and the buyer gets it |
| − Non-operating assets | Subtract | Investments that don't produce operating profit |

The trick questions test whether you understand the logic or memorized a formula:

- **A company borrows $100 and holds it as cash.** EV doesn't change: debt rises by $100 and cash rises by $100.
- **A company uses $100 of cash to repay debt.** EV doesn't change.
- **A company issues $100 of stock and keeps the cash.** Equity value rises $100 and cash rises $100, so EV doesn't change.
- **A company pays a $50 dividend.** Cash falls $50 and, in theory, equity value falls $50, so EV doesn't change.
- **Why subtract cash but not inventory?** Inventory is part of operations and helps produce EBITDA; excess cash doesn't.

## In the real world
Microsoft's 2022 agreement to buy Activision Blizzard is a clean example of the bridge in a press release. Activision shareholders were to receive $95 a share in cash, and Microsoft described the deal as "valued at $68.7 billion, inclusive of Activision Blizzard's net cash." In other words, the payment to shareholders added up to more than $68.7 billion, and the headline value netted out the cash Microsoft would get along with the company. When the next big deal is announced, read the release and find which number is equity value and which includes debt or cash.

## The interview trap
The trap is reciting the formula with no logic, or saying you subtract cash "because it's an asset." So is a factory, and you don't subtract that.

> Enterprise value is the value of the core business to everyone with a claim on it. If you bought the whole company, you'd pay the shareholders the equity value, but you'd also have to take on or repay its debt, so you add debt, along with other investor claims like preferred stock and noncontrolling interests. You subtract cash because it isn't part of the operating business, and you'd get it along with the company; you could use it to pay down debt, so it reduces your effective cost. That's also why EV doesn't change when a company borrows money and holds it as cash: debt goes up and cash goes up by the same amount.

## Your line
"Add every claim on the business, and subtract everything that isn't the business." **Follow-up you'll get:** "A company borrows $100 to build a $100 factory. What happens to EV?" **The other side:** Some analysts also add debt-like items such as lease obligations and pension deficits; what matters is staying consistent with the EBITDA you pair EV with.

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Nothing happens to EV: debt rises by $100 and cash rises by $100, so they offset, and equity value doesn't change either. It's a financing decision, not a change to the operating business.
- **a.** The parent reports 100% of a majority-owned subsidiary's revenue and EBITDA even though it owns less than all of it, so you add the outside owners' stake to make EV match the EBITDA.
- **b.** 650.
- **c.** In theory, nothing: cash falls by $30 and equity value falls by $30.
- **Rewind:** A house worth $500,000 with a $400,000 mortgage has $100,000 of equity; buying it means paying the owner $100,000 and taking on the mortgage, so the total cost is $500,000.
- **Brain teaser:** 1,225. For numbers ending in 5: 3 × 4 = 12, then add 25.
