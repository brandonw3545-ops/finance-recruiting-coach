# Risk: volatility, drawdowns, and what "risk tolerance" really means
*Comes up in:* Wealth management and asset management interviews, and any "how would you invest this client's money?" question.

## Cold open
"A client says they have a high risk tolerance. How do you find out if that's true?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
Risk is the chance that an investment leaves you with less than you needed, when you needed it. The industry measures it two ways. **Volatility** is how much returns bounce around, measured as a standard deviation. A **drawdown** is how far an investment falls from its high point before it recovers. Volatility is what textbooks use. Drawdowns are what clients feel.

"Risk tolerance" is really two separate things. **Willingness** is how much loss a client can stomach without panicking. **Capacity** is how much loss their plan can survive. A 25-year-old with a steady job has high capacity even if a falling account makes them nervous. A 64-year-old who retires next year has low capacity even if they love risk.

## How it works
Start with the arithmetic of losses, because it isn't symmetric:

| You lose | You need this gain to get back to even |
|---|---|
| 10% | 11% |
| 20% | 25% |
| 50% | 100% |

A portfolio that falls from $100 to $50 has to double just to get back to $100. That's why avoiding deep drawdowns matters more than chasing the last bit of return.

Volatility gives a rough range. If a stock fund averages 8% a year with a standard deviation of 16%, then in about two years out of three its return lands between −8% and +24%. One year in three, it lands outside that.

An advisor weighs three things before recommending anything:

- **Time horizon:** money needed in two years can't sit in stocks. Money needed in thirty years can.
- **Capacity:** income, savings, debts and how much of the portfolio the client will need to spend.
- **Willingness:** how they've actually behaved in past declines, which is more reliable than what they say on a form.

When capacity and willingness disagree, the lower one wins.

## In the real world
From October 2007 to March 2009, the S&P 500 fell about 57%. It didn't get back to its old high until 2013. Many investors who had described themselves as aggressive sold near the bottom and locked in the loss. Their willingness turned out to be lower than the questionnaire said. On a campus scale: if your student fund or paper portfolio fell 30% this semester, would you hold, or would you want out? Your honest answer is your willingness.

## The interview trap
The trap is treating risk as one number, or taking a client's word for their tolerance.

> I'd separate willingness from capacity. Capacity is financial: their time horizon, income, and how much of this money they'll need and when. Willingness is behavioral: how they actually reacted in 2008, 2020 or 2022, not how they answer a questionnaire in a calm market. Then I'd make it concrete, in dollars. Not "are you comfortable with volatility" but "this portfolio could be down $200,000 in a bad year. Would you stay invested?" If the two disagree, I'd build to the lower one, because a portfolio a client abandons at the bottom is worse than a cautious one they keep.

## Your line
"Risk tolerance is what a client does in a drawdown, not what they say before one." **Follow-up you'll get:** "What's the difference between risk tolerance and risk capacity?" **The other side:** Some argue volatility isn't risk at all for a long-term investor, and that the real risk is being too cautious and running out of money.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** A 30-year-old client wants everything in cash because markets scare them. What do you tell them?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. A portfolio falls 20%. What gain gets it back to even?
   b. What's the difference between willingness and capacity?
   c. What does a standard deviation of 16% tell you?
3. **Rewind:** Which kind of risk can diversification remove, and which kind can't it?
4. **Brain teaser:** A stock falls 50%, then rises 50%. Where does it end up, as a percent of where it started?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Acknowledge the fear, then show the other risk: over thirty years, inflation eats cash, so being too safe can mean not reaching the goal. They have high capacity and low willingness, so a strong answer proposes a middle path, like a balanced mix or investing gradually, that they can actually stick with.
- **a.** 25%.
- **b.** Willingness is how much loss a client can stomach emotionally. Capacity is how much loss their finances and timeline can absorb.
- **c.** In roughly two years out of three, returns land within 16 percentage points of the average.
- **Rewind:** It can remove company-specific risk. It can't remove market risk.
- **Brain teaser:** 75%.
