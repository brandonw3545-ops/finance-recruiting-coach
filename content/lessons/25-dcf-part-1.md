# DCF, part 1: the big idea and projecting free cash flow
*Comes up in:* Every banking, private equity and equity research process, and many asset management interviews.

## Cold open
"What's the idea behind a DCF?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
A company is worth the cash it will produce in the future, adjusted for the fact that future cash is worth less than cash today. A discounted cash flow analysis (DCF) projects the company's free cash flow for several years, estimates the value of everything after that, and discounts it all back to today. Think of valuing an apple orchard: it's worth the apples it will grow over the years, discounted because you have to wait for them and because some years the harvest will disappoint.

## How it works
Part one is the projection. You build unlevered free cash flow year by year, usually for five to ten years:

1. **Revenue:** start from history, the industry's growth and analysts' estimates.
2. **Operating margin:** how much of each dollar of revenue becomes operating income (EBIT).
3. **Taxes:** EBIT × (1 − tax rate).
4. **Add back** depreciation and amortization.
5. **Subtract** capital expenditures and the increase in working capital, the investment growth requires.

Here's an example: revenue of 1,000 growing 10% a year, a 20% EBIT margin, a 25% tax rate, D&A at 5% of revenue, capex at 6% of revenue, and working capital investment equal to 10% of each year's revenue increase.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,100 | 1,210 | 1,331 | 1,464 | 1,611 |
| EBIT (20%) | 220 | 242 | 266 | 293 | 322 |
| After-tax EBIT | 165 | 182 | 200 | 220 | 242 |
| **Unlevered free cash flow** | **144.0** | **158.4** | **174.2** | **191.7** | **210.8** |

In year 1, for instance: 165 of after-tax EBIT, plus 55 of D&A, minus 66 of capex, minus 10 of working capital investment, equals 144. Parts two and three, the discount rate and the terminal value, turn these cash flows into a value.

The big judgment calls are revenue growth, margins and how much reinvestment that growth needs. Small changes compound over the years, which is why the assumptions matter more than the spreadsheet.

## In the real world
In June 2014, NYU professor Aswath Damodaran published a DCF valuing Uber at about $5.9 billion, at the same moment investors were putting money in at a $17 billion valuation. Bill Gurley, an Uber investor, replied that Damodaran had assumed far too small a market: Uber could grow the market for rides, not just take share of the taxi business. Same method, very different assumptions, very different answers, and Uber went on to be worth far more than either number. Damodaran's blog, "Musings on Markets," posts full valuations with every assumption shown, and it's one of the best free ways to learn the craft.

## The interview trap
The trap is jumping straight to formulas without explaining what a DCF is trying to capture, or being vague about which cash flow you project.

> A DCF values a company based on the present value of the cash it will generate. You project the company's unlevered free cash flow, usually for five to ten years, starting with revenue growth and margins, then subtracting taxes, capex and the investment in working capital, while adding back depreciation. Because the company keeps going after the projection period, you estimate a terminal value for everything beyond it. Then you discount the projected cash flows and the terminal value back to today at the weighted average cost of capital, which reflects the risk and the time value of money. The sum is the enterprise value; subtract net debt to get equity value, and divide by diluted shares to get a value per share.

## Your line
"A DCF is only as good as its assumptions, and that's exactly why it's useful: it forces you to say them out loud." **Follow-up you'll get:** "What are the most important assumptions in a DCF?" **The other side:** Critics say you can get almost any answer by tweaking growth and the discount rate, which is why good analysts show sensitivity tables.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** Why might two analysts get very different DCF values for the same company?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Revenue is 1,000, the EBIT margin is 20%, the tax rate is 25%, D&A is 40, capex is 60 and working capital rises by 10. What's unlevered free cash flow?
   b. Why project five to ten years instead of thirty?
   c. Which discount rate goes with unlevered free cash flow?
3. **Rewind:** What's the formula for unlevered free cash flow?
4. **Brain teaser:** Revenue grows 10% a year for two years. What's the total growth?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** They can assume different revenue growth, margins, reinvestment, discount rates and terminal values, and small differences compound over many years. A strong answer adds that this is why you show sensitivity tables and cross-check with comps.
- **a.** 120: EBIT of 200 × 0.75 = 150, plus 40, minus 60, minus 10.
- **b.** Forecasts become unreliable after five to ten years; the terminal value captures everything after that.
- **c.** The weighted average cost of capital (WACC).
- **Rewind:** EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − capex − increase in net working capital.
- **Brain teaser:** 21%: 1.1 × 1.1 = 1.21.
