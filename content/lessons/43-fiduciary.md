# Fiduciary vs. suitability: who has to put the client first
*Comes up in:* Wealth management interviews, ethics questions, and "what would you do?" scenarios.

## Cold open
"What does it mean to be a fiduciary?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
A **fiduciary** is legally required to act in the client's best interest and to put that interest ahead of their own. Not everyone who gives financial advice is held to that standard. Which one applies depends on how the person is registered, and the difference decides what they're allowed to recommend when two products would both work but one pays them more.

## How it works
There are two kinds of registration in the United States:

| | Investment adviser | Broker-dealer |
|---|---|---|
| Main law | Investment Advisers Act of 1940 | Securities Exchange Act of 1934 |
| Standard | Fiduciary: loyalty and care, all the time | Best interest, at the moment of a recommendation |
| Usually paid | A fee on assets | Commissions |
| Ongoing duty | Yes, for the whole relationship | Generally no duty to keep monitoring |

For decades, brokers only had to meet a **suitability** standard: a recommendation had to fit the client's situation, even if a cheaper alternative existed. Since June 2020, the SEC's **Regulation Best Interest** has required brokers to act in a retail customer's best interest when they recommend something, and to disclose and manage their conflicts. It's stricter than suitability, but it applies at the point of sale, not continuously.

Many advisors at large firms are registered both ways. They wear the fiduciary hat on fee-based accounts and the broker hat on commission accounts, which is why the first question a careful client asks is "are you acting as a fiduciary on this account?"

A simple test: two funds track the same index. One costs 0.05% a year and pays the advisor nothing. The other costs 1% and pays a commission. Under the old suitability rule, either could be recommended. A fiduciary would have a very hard time justifying the second.

## In the real world
In 2016, the Department of Labor finalized a rule that would have made anyone advising on retirement accounts a fiduciary. The industry sued, and in 2018 a federal appeals court threw the rule out. The SEC's Regulation Best Interest followed in 2019 and took effect in 2020. The fight shows how much money rides on this one word. When you talk to an advisor at an info session, ask how their firm handles the two standards. It's a good question and few students ask it.

## The interview trap
The trap is saying all advisors are fiduciaries, or implying brokers can sell whatever they like.

> A fiduciary has a legal duty of loyalty and care: they have to put the client's interest ahead of their own, all the time. Registered investment advisers are fiduciaries under the Advisers Act of 1940. Brokers used to be held only to suitability, meaning a recommendation had to fit the client. Since 2020, Regulation Best Interest requires brokers to act in a retail client's best interest when they make a recommendation and to manage conflicts, but it applies at the moment of the recommendation, not across the whole relationship. Many advisors are registered as both, so the standard depends on the account.

## Your line
"The standard follows the account, not the business card." **Follow-up you'll get:** "A product pays you more but a cheaper one does the same job. What do you do?" **The other side:** Some argue commissions serve small investors who trade rarely better than an ongoing fee would, so a single fiduciary standard could price them out of advice.

## Your turn
1. **Say it out loud:** A long-time client asks you to buy a risky stock that doesn't fit their plan. What do you do?
2. **Rapid fire:**
   a. Which law makes investment advisers fiduciaries?
   b. What replaced suitability for brokers' retail recommendations?
   c. What are the two duties a fiduciary owes?
3. **Rewind:** How does a fee on assets line an advisor up with the client, and where does it not?
4. **Brain teaser:** A fund charges 1% a year and another charges 0.05%. On $100,000, what's the difference in fees in one year?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** Explain the risk and how it conflicts with their goals, recommend against it or suggest a small position, and document the conversation. It's the client's money and decision, but a strong answer shows you'd advise honestly first and wouldn't just take the order.
- **a.** The Investment Advisers Act of 1940.
- **b.** Regulation Best Interest.
- **c.** Loyalty and care.
- **Rewind:** The advisor earns more when the account grows. It doesn't line up when the client's best move takes money out, like paying off a mortgage.
- **Brain teaser:** $950.
