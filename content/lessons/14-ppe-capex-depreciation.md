# PP&E, capex and depreciation
*Comes up in:* Accounting technicals, and any discussion of capital-heavy businesses, from airlines to the companies building AI data centers.

## Cold open
"What's the difference between capex and depreciation?"

Answer it out loud before you read on.

## The big idea
When a company buys a long-lived asset, like a building, a machine or a server, it pays cash up front. That's **capital expenditure (capex)**. But the asset helps produce revenue for years, so accounting spreads its cost across those years as **depreciation**. Think of a $3,000 laptop you'll use for three years: you paid the full $3,000 today, but you "use up" about $1,000 of it each year.

## How it works
Where each one shows up:

- **Capex:** the cash flow statement, under investing, and an increase in PP&E on the balance sheet. It never hits the income statement directly.
- **Depreciation:** an expense on the income statement that lowers operating income and taxes. It uses no cash, so it's added back on the cash flow statement, and it reduces PP&E on the balance sheet.

Straight-line depreciation is the cost minus any salvage value, divided by the useful life. A $100 machine with a five-year life and no salvage value depreciates $20 a year:

| Year | Depreciation | Net PP&E at year end |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 80 |
| 2 | 20 | 60 |
| 3 | 20 | 40 |
| 4 | 20 | 20 |
| 5 | 20 | 0 |

In year one, with a 25% tax rate: depreciation of 20 lowers pre-tax income by 20 and net income by 15. On the cash flow statement, net income is down 15, depreciation adds back 20, and capex is −100. Over five years, total depreciation equals the $100 paid. **Amortization** is the same idea for intangible assets, like patents.

Two more terms: **maintenance capex** keeps existing assets running, and **growth capex** adds capacity. When capex runs well above depreciation, a company is growing its asset base; when it runs below for long, it may be underinvesting.

## In the real world
The AI build-out has made this lesson front-page news. In 2024, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta together spent more than $200 billion on capex, mostly data centers and chips. That cash went out right away, but the depreciation will hit their income statements for years, which is why investors watch both. Useful life matters too: in 2022 Microsoft extended the estimated life of its servers from four years to six, which cut annual depreciation and raised reported operating income by billions of dollars without changing its cash at all. On the next Big Tech earnings call, listen for capex guidance and any mention of depreciation.

## The interview trap
The trap is calling depreciation a cash expense, or saying capex hits the income statement right away.

> Capex is the cash a company spends to buy or upgrade long-lived assets like buildings and equipment. It shows up on the cash flow statement under investing and adds to PP&E on the balance sheet, but it doesn't hit the income statement right away. Depreciation is how that cost gets expensed over the asset's useful life: it reduces operating income and taxes each year, but it's a non-cash expense, so it's added back on the cash flow statement. Over an asset's life, total depreciation equals what the company paid, minus any salvage value. Comparing the two is useful too: if capex runs well above depreciation, the company is growing its asset base; if it's below, it may be underinvesting.

## Your line
"Capex is when you pay; depreciation is when you expense it." **Follow-up you'll get:** "What happens to earnings if a company extends the useful life of its assets?" **The other side:** Some argue depreciation understates true costs during inflation, because replacing an asset costs more than its historical price.

   b. What's the difference between maintenance capex and growth capex?
   c. How is amortization different from depreciation?
3. **Rewind:** How does net income link to the balance sheet?

## Answers
- **Say it out loud:** On the day of purchase, there's no income statement effect. On the cash flow statement, capex of −100 appears under investing. On the balance sheet, cash falls by 100 and PP&E rises by 100, so total assets don't change. Depreciation starts afterward.
- **a.** $10 a year.
- **b.** Maintenance capex keeps existing assets running; growth capex adds new capacity.
- **c.** It's the same idea applied to intangible assets, like patents or acquired customer relationships.
- **Rewind:** Net income, minus dividends, adds to retained earnings in shareholders' equity.
- **Brain teaser:** 22 times: 11 times in each 12-hour period, because the hands meet every 12/11 of an hour.
